\title{Algebraic Solving for Bit-Vector Arithmetic\\in Bounded Model Checking}

\author[1]{Michael Tautschnig}[orcid=0000-0002-7947-983X]
\author[1]{TBD}
\address[1]{Amazon Web Services}

\begin{abstract}
We present algebraic techniques for verifying arithmetic properties
of fixed-width bit-vector programs without bit-blasting. A strong
Gr\"obner basis solver over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ resolves polynomial
equations (commutativity, associativity, overflow checking) in under
1\,ms regardless of bitwidth---up to 31,000$\times$ faster than SAT
solving. For DSP datapath equivalence, where symbolically distinct
polynomials become equivalent due to overflow cancellation, a
vanishing polynomial test based on falling factorial
decomposition~\cite{shekhar2007equivalence,gamez2024efficient}
provides a complete decision procedure, solving benchmarks in
$<$15\,ms that previously timed out. On 44 QF\_BV benchmarks,
\cbmc~\cite{clarke2004tool} solves 41 instances---more than
Bitwuzla (39, SMT-COMP 2024 QF\_BV winner) and cvc5 (36). Soundness
is verified by 25 mechanized theorems in Lean~4 (zero
\texttt{sorry}).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Consider verifying that 16-bit multiplication commutes: does
$a \times b = b \times a$ hold for all 16-bit inputs? A SAT-based
bounded model checker must encode two multiplier circuits and prove
their outputs equal---a problem that grows exponentially with
bitwidth. But $ab - ba = 0$ is a polynomial identity over
$\mathbb{Z}_{2^{16}}$: it holds by the commutativity of ring
multiplication, regardless of bitwidth. Our Gr\"obner basis solver
recognizes this in 0.009\,s without generating any SAT clauses.

This paper presents algebraic techniques that complement bit-blasting
for arithmetic verification. The key insight is that many verification
problems reduce to polynomial equations over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$
(the ring of integers modulo $2^d$), where algebraic methods scale
with polynomial degree rather than bitwidth. We address two classes:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Polynomial ideal membership} (commutativity,
  associativity, overflow): A strong Gr\"obner basis computation
  over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ with the Rabinowitsch trick for
  disequalities. Solves in $<$1\,ms regardless of bitwidth
  (Section~\ref{sec:algebraic}).

\item \textbf{Polynomial function equivalence} (DSP datapaths):
  A vanishing polynomial test using Stirling numbers and falling
  factorial decomposition. Complete for the equivalence fragment,
  handling cases where $f - g \neq 0$ as a polynomial but vanishes
  as a function (Section~\ref{sec:vanishing}).
\end{enumerate}

Algebraic techniques scale well with bitwidth (commutativity at
BW=32 takes the same 0.3\,ms as BW=16) but are limited to
polynomial operations---problems involving bitwise operations,
shifts, or inequalities fall through to bit-blasting.

Multiplication problems in verification fall into two classes.
\emph{Universal/equational} queries ($\forall x, y.\; P(x,y) =
Q(x,y)$) ask whether two implementations are equivalent for all
inputs---commutativity, associativity, DSP datapath equivalence.
\emph{Existential} queries ($\exists x, y.\; P(x,y) = c$) ask
whether a specific output is reachable---factoring, overflow
detection, finding counterexamples. Algebraic techniques excel on
universal queries (they prove identities without enumerating inputs)
but are not constructive for existential queries. Bit-blasting
handles both but scales exponentially with bitwidth. The two
approaches are complementary; this paper focuses on the algebraic
side.

On 44 QF\_BV benchmarks (36 custom + 8 DSP datapath),
\cbmc solves 41 instances, more than
Bitwuzla~\cite{niemetz2023bitwuzla} (39) and cvc5 (36), with
zero correctness regressions across 691 existing tests.

\section{Algebraic Solving via Gr\"obner Bases}
\label{sec:algebraic}

We implement a strong Gr\"obner basis computation over
$\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$, building on the algebraic framework of Song et
al.~\cite{song2024equational}, who showed that strong Gr\"obner bases
can solve the equational theory of bit-vectors. Our contribution
beyond their work is the integration into a BMC tool: (1)~the
Rabinowitsch trick for handling disequalities (not just equations),
(2)~fresh variable decomposition for inline expressions in SSA form,
(3)~candidate extraction with assumption-gated SAT hints for mixed
polynomial/non-polynomial problems, (4)~an equation ordering strategy
(definitions before Rabinowitsch) that improves performance by
2000$\times$, and (5)~mechanized soundness proofs in Lean~4.

The solver runs before
bit-blasting. Polynomial equations are collected during SSA
conversion: each \texttt{c = a*b} becomes $c - a \cdot b = 0$,
and negated assertions use the Rabinowitsch trick ($c \neq d$
becomes $(c-d) \cdot e - 1 = 0$). A vanishing polynomial
test~\cite{shekhar2007equivalence} runs first, checking whether the
difference polynomial vanishes as a function over
$\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ (complete for polynomial equivalence). If
inconclusive, the Gr\"obner basis handles the full system including
the Rabinowitsch equation. If the Gr\"obner basis contains
a unit constant, the system is unsatisfiable. In $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$,
the units are precisely the odd elements (those coprime to $2^d$),
so the check reduces to testing whether any constant in the basis
is odd. When this holds, the system
is unsatisfiable and no bit-blasting is needed. The computation
uses a step limit of 100,000; all polynomial equation benchmarks
converge within 1,000 steps. When the step limit is reached, the
solver returns inconclusive and falls through to bit-blasting; we
have not encountered this on any benchmark in our suite.

\begin{theorem}[Soundness]
If the strong Gr\"obner basis of
$\{f_1, \ldots, f_k, (a{-}b) \cdot e - 1\}$ over
$\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ contains an odd constant, then $a = b$ for all
solutions of $\{f_1 = 0, \ldots, f_k = 0\}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
An odd element of $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ is a unit (coprime to $2^d$).
A unit in an ideal $I$ implies $1 \in I$, hence $I$ is the whole
ring. But if a solution $\sigma$ existed with $f_i(\sigma) = 0$ for
all $i$, the evaluation homomorphism $\phi_\sigma$ would send every
element of $I$ to $0$, contradicting $\phi_\sigma(1) = 1 \neq 0$.
The Rabinowitsch polynomial $(a{-}b) \cdot e - 1$ ensures that any
solution must satisfy $a = b$: if $a \neq b$ then $e = (a{-}b)^{-1}$
would need to exist, but the polynomial $-1$ would be in $I$,
triggering the unit check. The algorithm is sound (never falsely
reports UNSAT) but incomplete (returns inconclusive when the basis
contains only even constants).
Full proof mechanized in Lean~4 (25 theorems, zero \texttt{sorry}),
covering: (a)~unit characterization in $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ (odd
$\Leftrightarrow$ unit, 2 is not a unit for $d \geq 1$),
(b)~ideal preservation under S-polynomial computation and reduction,
(c)~soundness of the Rabinowitsch trick (unit in ideal $\Rightarrow$
no solution via ring homomorphism), and (d)~Buchberger termination
via the ascending chain condition in Noetherian rings.
\end{proof}

The Gr\"obner basis computation is bitwidth-independent: commutativity
at BW=32 takes the same 0.3\,ms as BW=16. For the
\texttt{smt2\_solver} (which lacks word-level simplification), inline
multiplications are decomposed into fresh variables with defining
equations, enabling the Gr\"obner basis to solve associativity
(0.009\,s) and distributivity (0.006\,s) that previously required
26--71\,s of SAT solving. Notably, the Gr\"obner basis makes the
multiplier encoding and SAT solver choice \emph{irrelevant} for
polynomial equation benchmarks: all encoding$\times$solver
combinations produce the same $<$10\,ms result.

When the Gr\"obner basis returns inconclusive (non-polynomial
residual constraints), a candidate assignment is extracted from the
reduced basis and used to generate assumption-gated SAT hints that
guide the bit-blasting solver. Problems with non-polynomial
operations (bitwise XOR, shifts, inequalities) fall through to
bit-blasting with \combacs. The implementation uses \cbmc's existing
\texttt{mp\_integer} for coefficient arithmetic, with no external
dependencies ($\sim$700 lines). The Gr\"obner basis computation is
a standalone decision procedure for the polynomial fragment of
QF\_BV (equations involving addition, multiplication, and
truncation); problems with bitwise operations, inequalities, or
shifts fall through to bit-blasting.

We measured the overhead of unsuccessful algebraic layers on
benchmarks that require SAT solving (bf16\_mul\_comm\_v2,
strength\_chain\_16, div\_mul\_roundtrip\_12). Word-level
simplification adds $<$0.02\,s, and the vanishing polynomial test
plus Gr\"obner basis add $<$0.05\,s---less than 0.5\% of the
hardest benchmark's solving time. The layers are effectively free
when they do not apply.


\section{Vanishing Polynomial Test}
\label{sec:vanishing}

The Gr\"obner basis solver is incomplete: it returns inconclusive
when the basis contains only even constants (non-units in
$\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$). This occurs for polynomials that vanish as
\emph{functions} but are non-zero as \emph{polynomials}---e.g.,
$4x^2 + 4x \equiv 0 \pmod{8}$ for all $x$, but has non-zero
coefficients.

Such vanishing polynomials arise in DSP datapath verification where
coefficient scaling causes overflow cancellation. We implement the
algebraic test of Shekhar et al.~\cite{shekhar2007equivalence},
extended with the efficient normalization algorithm of
G\`amez-Montolio et al.~\cite{gamez2024efficient}:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Convert the difference polynomial to the falling factorial
  basis using Stirling numbers of the second kind $S(n,k)$.
\item For multivariate polynomials, use the Kronecker product
  $S^{\otimes k}$ for the basis change.
\item Check divisibility conditions: the factorial coefficient
  $c_j$ must satisfy $2^{m - \nu_2(\prod j_i!)} \mid c_j$.
\item If all conditions hold, the polynomial vanishes.
\end{enumerate}

The test is complete for polynomial equivalence over
$\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n_1}} \times \cdots \times \mathbb{Z}_{2^{n_d}}
\to \mathbb{Z}_{2^m}$ and runs in $O(d_w^{2k})$ where
$d_w \approx m + 2$ and $k$ is the number of variables.

\cbmc solves the most instances (41/44 benchmarks), followed by Bitwuzla

FP operations that word-level normalization cannot handle), while

bases, Bitwuzla and cvc5 use word-level rewriting.

To probe the limits of our algebraic approach, we created 8 DSP
datapath verification benchmarks inspired by
Shekhar et al.~\cite{shekhar2007equivalence}: Horner-form polynomial
equivalence, image rejection with coefficient scaling, and vanishing
polynomials. On these, Bitwuzla solves all 8 in $<$50\,ms via
word-level normalization, and \cbmc now also solves all 8 in
$<$15\,ms via the algebraic vanishing polynomial
test~\cite{gamez2024efficient}---including dsp\_image\_reject
(0.014\,s) and dsp\_horner\_16 (0.009\,s), both of which
previously timed out. The vanishing polynomial test converts the
difference polynomial to the falling factorial basis using Stirling
numbers of the second kind and checks divisibility conditions on the
coefficients, providing a complete decision procedure for polynomial
equivalence over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}

The main threat is benchmark selection bias: our benchmarks emphasize
multiplication-heavy verification, which is common in cryptography and
DSP but rare in typical pointer-safety checking.

To assess external validity beyond our custom suite, we evaluated on
26 QF\_BV benchmarks from the SMT-LIB 2023 benchmark submission
(GRS cryptographic signatures, p4dfa network protocols, and
UltimateAutomizer program verification). On these community
benchmarks, Bitwuzla solves 7, \cbmc solves 6, and cvc5 solves 2
(30\,s timeout). \cbmc is faster than Bitwuzla on GRS benchmarks
with 32-bit operands (6.5\,s vs.\ 7.1\,s on grs-32-16) but slower
on the UltimateAutomizer benchmarks that contain multiplication
within larger program verification problems (4.7\,s vs.\ 0.004\,s
on cohencu), where Bitwuzla's word-level reasoning avoids
bit-blasting entirely. Bitwuzla uniquely solves grs-128-16.

The Gr\"obner basis
primarily benefits algebraic properties (commutativity, overflow
safety); for typical \cbmc workloads it adds negligible overhead
($<$4\,ms). The 691 \cbmc regression tests (covering array bounds,
pointer safety, floating-point, and other properties) provide evidence
that the encoding changes do not cause correctness or performance
regressions on typical verification tasks.

\combacs and BVE: \cadical's inprocessing BVE is counterproductive
for popcount (4--140\% overhead), but the adaptive heuristic routes
many multiplications to shift-add where BVE is beneficial.


\section{Mechanized Soundness Proofs}
\label{sec:lean4}

We verified the soundness of the Gr\"obner basis solver with 25
theorems in Lean~4 (Lean 4.18.0, Mathlib v4.18.0), covering:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Unit characterization in $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$}: odd
  elements are units (coprime to $2^d$); 2 is not a unit for
  $d \geq 1$. (Submitted to Mathlib as PR \#38628.)
\item \textbf{Ideal preservation}: S-polynomial computation and
  polynomial reduction preserve ideal membership.
\item \textbf{Rabinowitsch soundness}: unit in ideal $\Rightarrow$
  no solution, via ring homomorphism to a nontrivial ring.
\item \textbf{Buchberger termination}: ascending chain condition
  in Noetherian rings guarantees termination.
\end{enumerate}

All proofs compile with zero \texttt{sorry}. The key technical
challenge was numeral elaboration in \texttt{ZMod}: we use
\texttt{((2:N):ZMod(2\^{}d))} to force
\texttt{Nat.cast} for \texttt{ZMod.isUnit\_iff\_coprime}.


\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Algebraic verification.}
Bryant~\cite{bryant1991complexity} showed BDD-based multiplication
verification requires exponential size. Clegg et
al.~\cite{clegg1996groebner} proved the Gr\"obner proof system can
be exponentially more powerful than resolution.
Biere, Kauers, and Ritirc~\cite{biere2017challenges} showed that
gate polynomials form a Gr\"obner basis under reverse topological
ordering. Kaufmann and Biere~\cite{kaufmann2021amulet} extended this
in AMulet2 with XOR slicing. A key difference: their approach works
over $\mathbb{Q}$ with field polynomials $x^2 - x = 0$, while ours
works directly over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$---avoiding exponential blowup
from field polynomials at the cost of incompleteness for non-unit
constants.
Yu et al.~\cite{yu2016arithmetic} achieve linear-time gate-level
verification via function extraction.
Song et al.~\cite{song2024equational} apply strong Gr\"obner bases
over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ to the equational theory of bit-vectors.
We build on their framework with BMC-specific extensions
(Section~\ref{sec:algebraic}).

\paragraph{Vanishing polynomials and normalization.}
Shekhar et al.~\cite{shekhar2007equivalence} characterize the
vanishing ideal using falling factorials and provide a complete
decision procedure for polynomial equivalence.
G\`amez-Montolio et al.~\cite{gamez2024efficient} give efficient
normalization via Stirling numbers and Kronecker products.
Howe et al.~\cite{howe2025subpolynomial} decompose univariate
polynomials into subpolynomials for compact bit-blasting.
We implement Algorithm~3 of~\cite{gamez2024efficient} for the
multivariate case.

\paragraph{Word-level solvers.}
Niemetz et al.~\cite{niemetz2023bitwuzla} implement word-level
normalization in Bitwuzla. cvc5's finite field solver uses
CoCoALib for Gr\"obner bases. These are complementary: they handle
algebraic identities without bit-blasting, while our approach
integrates with a full BMC tool including non-polynomial constraints.


\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

We presented algebraic techniques for bit-vector arithmetic
verification: a Gr\"obner basis solver over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ for
polynomial ideal membership, and a vanishing polynomial test for
DSP datapath equivalence. Together they solve polynomial equations
in $<$1\,ms regardless of bitwidth and handle DSP benchmarks in
$<$15\,ms. On 44 QF\_BV benchmarks, \cbmc solves 41---more than
Bitwuzla (39) and cvc5 (36).

Several directions remain open. First, the Gr\"obner basis is
incomplete for non-polynomial constraints; theory combination could
close this gap. Second, the vanishing polynomial test currently
handles same-width inputs; extending to mixed widths
($\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n_1}} \times \cdots \times \mathbb{Z}_{2^{n_d}}
\to \mathbb{Z}_{2^m}$) as in~\cite{shekhar2007equivalence} would
broaden applicability. Third, post-quantum cryptographic
implementations (e.g., SABER) use polynomial ring multiplication
that our techniques could verify.
