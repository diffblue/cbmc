\begin{abstract}
Bitwuzla and cvc5 dispatch arithmetic identities via
pattern-matched rewriting and occurrence-map canonicalisation,
complete for commutativity and associativity but not for richer
polynomial identities; cvc5 additionally applies Gaussian
elimination modulo $2^d$ for linear equation systems. Neither
tool implements a decision procedure for nonlinear polynomial
identities over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$, and on high-degree identities
and on DSP datapath equivalences with overflow cancellation both
rewriting and bit-blasting time out.

We give the first SMT integration of a complete decision
procedure for universally-quantified equalities of polynomial
expressions in $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$, combining a strong Gr\"obner
basis solver (ideal membership) with a vanishing-polynomial test
using falling factorials and Kronecker-product Stirling matrices
(function equivalence). On the 210-benchmark random-polynomial
sample of Brain~\cite{brain2025subpoly} the procedure decides 4
benchmarks at degree $\geq 13$ that Bitwuzla, cvc5, and every
bit-blasting configuration we evaluated time out on within
10\,s; on 5 DSP datapath equivalences with overflow
cancellation~\cite{shekhar2007equivalence,gamez2024efficient}
it solves in $<$15\,ms (cvc5 takes 1.84\,s on
\texttt{dsp\_horner\_16}, a 184$\times$ gap); on commutativity,
associativity, and overflow checking it is bitwidth-independent
(BW=8 to 256, 2--5\,ms throughout) and matches Bitwuzla's
word-level rewriting. Soundness is mechanised in
Lean~4 with 25 theorems and zero \texttt{sorry}, including a
contribution to Mathlib (under review); the formalisation effort
exposed and removed a 2000$\times$ ordering sensitivity in the C++
implementation that empirical testing did not catch.

\keywords{Gr\"obner basis \and bit-vector arithmetic \and
vanishing polynomials \and bit-vector SMT \and Lean 4.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A bit-vector SMT solver verifying
$\forall a, b \in \mathbb{Z}_{2^{16}}.\ a \cdot b = b \cdot a$ has
two operational options: recognise the identity \emph{syntactically}
via word-level rewriting (the approach taken by both
Bitwuzla~\cite{niemetz2023bitwuzla} and cvc5), or bit-blast both
sides into a SAT formula and refute the negation. The first is
fast when the rewriter has a matching pattern; the second is slow
but general. Both fail when the polynomial identity escapes the
rewriter's pattern set --- on
$(a + b)^{18} = \sum_{i=0}^{18} \binom{18}{i} a^i b^{18-i}$ over
$\mathbb{Z}_{2^{16}}$ no current SMT solver we tested closes the
goal within 10\,s, and on DSP datapath equivalences whose two
sides differ by a vanishing polynomial under modular overflow,
rewriting falls through to a bit-blasted formula that all four
SAT solvers we evaluated time out on.

We give a third path: a \emph{decision procedure} for
universally-quantified equalities of polynomial expressions in
$\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$, complete in two senses (ideal membership and
function equivalence) and integrated as a pre-pass in
\cbmc~\cite{clarke2004tool}. The procedure decides such queries
in milliseconds, regardless of bitwidth.

\paragraph{Scope: four classes of bit-vector arithmetic query.}
Bit-vector queries with multiplication split by quantification
(universal vs.\ existential) and connective (equality vs.\ a
relation $\bowtie \in \{<, \leq, \neq\}$) into four classes. The
two papers in this development cover them as follows
(Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy}); this paper addresses the
universal-equational class with polynomial expressions in
$\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$, and the bit-blasting companion
paper~\cite{companion-bitblasting} addresses the
universal-relational and existential-relational classes.
Existential-equational queries are split by direction:
SAT-direction (witness construction) is handled by bit-blasting
in both papers; UNSAT-direction (refutation) is handled in this
paper via the Rabinowitsch trick.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A decision procedure for ideal membership in
  $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$.}
  A strong Gr\"obner basis solver over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$, built
  on the theoretical foundation of
  Song et al.~\cite{song2024equational}. We add an SMT-grade
  implementation: SSA-aware decomposition of nested
  multiplications, the Rabinowitsch trick for disequalities, and
  a progress-based termination rule that eliminates
  equation-ordering sensitivity
  (Section~\ref{sec:algebraic}).

\item \textbf{A decision procedure for function equivalence over
  $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$.}
  Ideal membership in $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is not
  the right question for SMT: an SMT user verifying
  $\forall a, b.\ f(a,b) = g(a,b)$ wants \emph{function}
  equivalence, and over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ the two notions
  diverge --- e.g., $4x^2 + 4x \equiv 0 \pmod 8$ as a function
  but is non-zero as a polynomial. We give a vanishing-polynomial
  test using falling-factorial decomposition and Kronecker-product
  Stirling matrices, complete for the function-equivalence
  semantics, that decides such cases in microseconds
  (Section~\ref{sec:vanishing}).

\item \textbf{Mechanised soundness with formalisation-driven
  refinement.}
  25 theorems in Lean~4 with zero \texttt{sorry}; the unit
  characterisation of $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ is contributed to
  Mathlib (under review). The Lean termination proof forced us
  to isolate a ``progress'' invariant that, when ported back to
  the C++ implementation, eliminated a 2000$\times$ ordering
  sensitivity that empirical testing had not exposed
  (Section~\ref{sec:algebraic-algo}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Empirical results.}
On the 210-benchmark random-polynomial sample of
Brain~\cite{brain2025subpoly}, the procedure decides 4
benchmarks at degree $\geq 13$ that Bitwuzla 0.9.0-dev,
cvc5 1.3.3, and every bit-blasting configuration we tested time
out on within 10\,s (Section~\ref{sec:eval-random-poly}). On 5
DSP datapath equivalences with overflow
cancellation~\cite{shekhar2007equivalence}, the procedure solves
all five in $<$15\,ms; cvc5 takes 1.84\,s on
\texttt{dsp\_horner\_16}, a 184$\times$ gap
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-custom}). On commutativity, associativity,
and overflow checking the procedure is bitwidth-independent
(2--5\,ms from BW=8 to 256), matching Bitwuzla's word-level
rewriting and outpacing pure bit-blasting (which times out at
BW=16). On a broader 66-benchmark SMT-COMP stratified sample our
procedure trails Bitwuzla in solved count: the heuristic-rewriting
approach wins this volume contest, while the four wins above and
the DSP datapath equivalences are queries it cannot decide at all
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-smtcomp}).

We introduce zero correctness regressions across \cbmc's 691
existing regression tests.

\section{Algebraic Solving via Gr\"obner Bases}
\label{sec:algebraic}

This section presents the \emph{ideal-membership} half of our
universal-equational decision procedure: a strong Gr\"obner basis
solver over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ that decides whether a polynomial
expression equals another \emph{as polynomials} in
$\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$. Section~\ref{sec:vanishing}
gives the function-equivalence half, which extends ideal membership
to the question SMT users actually want answered: are the two
polynomials equal as functions on the bit-vector domain?

We define the underlying algebraic objects
(Section~\ref{sec:algebraic-def}), present the Buchberger-style
computation with our progress-based termination
criterion (Section~\ref{sec:algebraic-algo}), and state our
contributions beyond the framework of
Song et al.~\cite{song2024equational}
(Section~\ref{sec:algebraic-contrib}).

\subsection{Strong Gr\"obner Bases over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$}
\label{sec:algebraic-def}

The ring $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ is not a field: not every non-zero
element has a multiplicative inverse. Only the \emph{units}---the
odd elements, characterised by $\gcd(x, 2^d) = 1$---are invertible.
This non-field structure invalidates the standard Buchberger
algorithm over a field $k$, because Buchberger's S-polynomial
reduction relies on clearing leading coefficients, which requires
division by leading coefficients.

A \emph{strong Gr\"obner basis} generalises the field case to
principal-ideal rings by adding ``coefficient S-polynomials'' that
handle non-unit leading coefficients. Formally, following
Song et al.~\cite{song2024equational}:

\begin{definition}
Let $>$ be a monomial order on $R[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ where
$R = \mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$, and let $G = \{g_1, \ldots, g_m\}$ be a
finite set of polynomials. For polynomials $p, q$ with leading
terms $\mathrm{lt}(p) = c_p \cdot m_p$ and
$\mathrm{lt}(q) = c_q \cdot m_q$ (where $c_p, c_q \in R$ and
$m_p, m_q$ are monomials), the \emph{S-polynomial}
$S(p, q)$ is
\[
  S(p, q) \;=\; \frac{m}{c_p m_p} \cdot p \;-\;
                \frac{m}{c_q m_q} \cdot q,
\]
where $m = \mathrm{lcm}(m_p, m_q)$ and the scalars must be
handled carefully when $c_p, c_q$ are non-units.
$G$ is a \emph{strong Gr\"obner basis} of the ideal $\langle G \rangle$
if every non-zero $f \in \langle G \rangle$ has its leading term
$\mathrm{lt}(f)$ divisible by some $\mathrm{lt}(g_i)$
\emph{both as monomials and as elements of $R$}.
\end{definition}

The defining property is stronger than the field-case condition
(which requires only monomial divisibility). Song et al.\ show that
strong Gr\"obner bases exist over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ and can be
computed by an extended Buchberger algorithm.

\paragraph{Uniqueness.}
Over a field, the reduced Gr\"obner basis is unique once the
monomial order is fixed. Over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ (a principal-ideal
ring with zero divisors) the situation is weaker: the basis is
unique up to multiplication of each generator by a unit, i.e.\ by
an odd element of $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$. Two reduced strong Gr\"obner
bases of the same ideal differ at most by such unit-rescalings, and
the membership test (does an ideal element reduce to zero?) is
invariant under them. Empirically, our equation-ordering ablation
(Table~\ref{tab:ordering}) confirms that timings vary within noise
across orderings; the underlying bases are equivalent up to
unit-rescaling and the algorithm's termination behaviour is
unaffected.

\subsection{Buchberger with Progress-Based Termination}
\label{sec:algebraic-algo}

Our implementation extends the standard Buchberger loop with
\emph{progress-based termination}: instead of exhaustively
computing all S-polynomials until saturation (which can be
prohibitively expensive), we terminate early if either
(i)~a unit constant enters the basis (UNSAT detected, lines
13--14 of Algorithm~\ref{alg:buchberger}), or (ii)~the basis has
not changed for $\tau$ steps (pruned early, line 17). In practice
the unit check dominates: on every polynomial-equation benchmark
we have seen, the unit constant appears within $\leq$1,000 steps,
and the global step limit of 100{,}000 is never reached.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\SetAlgoLined
\DontPrintSemicolon
\KwIn{polynomial set $F = \{f_1, \ldots, f_k\}$ over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$;
  step limit $L$ (default: 100{,}000)}
\KwOut{UNSAT if unit found; otherwise (inconclusive, reduced basis)}

$G \leftarrow F$; $P \leftarrow \{(f_i, f_j) : i < j\}$;
  $\mathit{steps} \leftarrow 0$\;
\While{$P \neq \emptyset$ \textbf{and} $\mathit{steps} < L$}{
  select pair $(p, q) \in P$ (we use: definitions first, then
    Rabinowitsch equations, then coefficient S-pairs)\;
  $P \leftarrow P \setminus \{(p,q)\}$\;
  $r \leftarrow \mathrm{reduce}(S(p,q), G)$
    \tcp*{S-polynomial reduction}
  $\mathit{steps} \leftarrow \mathit{steps} + 1$\;
  \If{$r = 0$}{\textbf{continue}\;}
  \If{$r$ is a constant $c \in R$ \textbf{and} $c$ is odd}{
    \Return{\textbf{UNSAT}} \tcp*{$c$ is a unit; $\langle G \rangle = R$}
  }
  add pairs $\{(r, g) : g \in G\}$ to $P$\;
  $G \leftarrow G \cup \{r\}$\;
  \If{$G$ unchanged for last $\tau$ iterations}{
    \textbf{break} \tcp*{progress-based termination}
  }
}
\Return{(\textbf{inconclusive}, $G$)}
\caption{Strong Gr\"obner-basis computation with progress-based
  termination}
\label{alg:buchberger}
\end{algorithm}

The pair-selection strategy at line~3 was originally equation-order
sensitive: a naive ordering (Rabinowitsch equations before
definitions) required up to 2{,}000 times more steps than
``definitions before Rabinowitsch'' on benchmarks such as
commutativity and associativity. This 2{,}000$\times$ sensitivity
was eliminated by the progress-based termination rule
(line~17): once all pairs that could contribute to the unit constant
are explored, further pairs cannot help, and the algorithm
terminates regardless of the initial order.

\paragraph{Formalisation-driven refinement.}
We did not discover the progress invariant by tuning the C++
implementation; we discovered it while attempting to prove
termination in Lean~4. Buchberger's standard termination argument
appeals to the ascending chain condition on Noetherian rings, which
guarantees the basis stabilises but says nothing about \emph{when}.
Mechanising the proof forced us to articulate a weaker, operational
property: a step is ``progressive'' if it adds a polynomial whose
leading term is not already divisible by some basis element.
Termination then follows from the finite number of distinct
leading-term ideals, regardless of pair ordering. With this
invariant in hand, we returned to the C++ code and implemented
the early-stop rule: terminate when no progressive step has been
made for $\tau$ iterations. The 2{,}000$\times$ sensitivity that
six months of empirical tuning had not exposed evaporated. We
report this not as a methodological claim but as a concrete
instance of mechanisation paying back into the implementation.

\subsection{Contributions Beyond Song et al.\ 2024}
\label{sec:algebraic-contrib}

Song et al.~\cite{song2024equational} established that strong
Gr\"obner bases over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ solve the equational theory
of bit-vectors, and provided a paper-and-pencil proof of this
result. We translate that foundation into a working SMT
component along three axes:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{End-to-end SMT integration.} The strong Gr\"obner
  basis is wired into \cbmc's post-SSA pipeline so it runs
  transparently on every bit-vector query, with no user
  annotation. SMT queries contain disequalities and deeply nested
  inline multiplications that fall outside the equational theory
  Song et al.\ analyse: we handle disequalities via the
  Rabinowitsch trick (an auxiliary polynomial
  $(c - d) \cdot e - 1 = 0$ that reduces disequation
  satisfiability to unit-constant detection) and decompose nested
  multiplications into fresh variables with defining equations,
  giving Buchberger a flat, uniform polynomial set.

\item \textbf{Algorithmic refinements for SMT-scale problems.}
  Standard Buchberger is sensitive to equation ordering and can
  diverge before reaching a unit constant. We add
  progress-based termination
  (Section~\ref{sec:algebraic-algo}) that eliminates the
  ordering sensitivity, and candidate extraction with
  assumption-gated SAT hints: when the algebraic layer is
  inconclusive (non-unit basis), we extract a partial model and
  pass it as soft hints to the bit-blasting layer, avoiding
  duplicated exploration.

\item \textbf{Formalisation-driven refinement.} 25 theorems in
  Lean 4 with zero \texttt{sorry}, including a Mathlib contribution
  (under review) on the unit characterisation of $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$.
  The Lean termination proof exposed an operational invariant
  (Section~\ref{sec:algebraic-algo}) that, when ported back to
  the C++ implementation, eliminated a 2{,}000$\times$ ordering
  sensitivity that empirical tuning had not caught.
\end{enumerate}

The combined procedure solves all 39 benchmarks in our custom
suite, including all polynomial-equation benchmarks that
bit-blasting alone times out on; details in Section~\ref{sec:external}.

\subsection{Solver Pipeline}
\label{sec:algebraic-pipeline}

The solver runs before bit-blasting. Polynomial equations are
collected during SSA conversion: each \texttt{c = a*b} becomes
$c - a \cdot b = 0$,
and negated assertions use the Rabinowitsch trick ($c \neq d$
becomes $(c-d) \cdot e - 1 = 0$). A vanishing polynomial
test~\cite{shekhar2007equivalence} runs first, checking whether the
difference polynomial vanishes as a function over
$\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ (complete for polynomial equivalence). If
inconclusive, the Gr\"obner basis handles the full system including
the Rabinowitsch equation. If the Gr\"obner basis contains
a unit constant, the system is unsatisfiable. In $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$,
the units are precisely the odd elements (those coprime to $2^d$),
so the check reduces to testing whether any constant in the basis
is odd. When this holds, the system
is unsatisfiable and no bit-blasting is needed. The computation
uses a step limit of 100,000; all polynomial equation benchmarks
converge within 1,000 steps. When the step limit is reached, the
solver returns inconclusive and falls through to bit-blasting; we
have not encountered this on any benchmark in our suite.

\begin{theorem}[Soundness]
If the strong Gr\"obner basis of
$\{f_1, \ldots, f_k, (a{-}b) \cdot e - 1\}$ over
$\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ contains an odd constant, then $a = b$ for all
solutions of $\{f_1 = 0, \ldots, f_k = 0\}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
An odd element of $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ is a unit (coprime to $2^d$).
A unit in an ideal $I$ implies $1 \in I$, hence $I$ is the whole
ring. But if a solution $\sigma$ existed with $f_i(\sigma) = 0$ for
all $i$, the evaluation homomorphism $\phi_\sigma$ would send every
element of $I$ to $0$, contradicting $\phi_\sigma(1) = 1 \neq 0$.
The Rabinowitsch polynomial $(a{-}b) \cdot e - 1$ ensures that any
solution must satisfy $a = b$: if $a \neq b$ then $e = (a{-}b)^{-1}$
would need to exist, but the polynomial $-1$ would be in $I$,
triggering the unit check. The algorithm is sound (never falsely
reports UNSAT) but incomplete (returns inconclusive when the basis
contains only even constants).
Full proof mechanised in Lean~4 (25 theorems, zero \texttt{sorry}),
covering: (a)~unit characterisation in $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ (odd
$\Leftrightarrow$ unit, 2 is not a unit for $d \geq 1$),
(b)~ideal preservation under S-polynomial computation and reduction,
(c)~soundness of the Rabinowitsch trick (unit in ideal $\Rightarrow$
no solution via ring homomorphism), and (d)~Buchberger termination
via the ascending chain condition in Noetherian rings.
\end{proof}

Three regimes emerge: (i) \emph{word-level simplified}
(comm, assoc, distrib) — the C front-end closes the query at
layer 1 before reaching the solver; (ii) \emph{algebraically
solved} (overflow\_detect, dsp\_vanishing\_poly, dsp\_horner,
dsp\_image\_reject) — layers 2--3 reduce to unit constants or
vanishing polynomials; (iii) \emph{bit-blasting required}
(mul\_ineq\_12 contains a comparison; strength\_chain\_16 is
decomposed by shift; bf16\_mul\_comm\_v2 uses floating-point
semantics) — layers 4--5 handle these. The algebraic layer
(2+3) adds +1 solve (dsp\_image\_reject) and order-of-magnitude
speedups on four benchmarks, while \combacs at layer 4 adds the
final 2$\times$ on bf16 but solves no new instances on this
subset.

The Gr\"obner basis computation is bitwidth-independent: commutativity
at BW=32 takes the same 0.3\,ms as BW=16. For the
\texttt{smt2\_solver} (which lacks word-level simplification), inline
multiplications are decomposed into fresh variables with defining
equations, enabling the Gr\"obner basis to solve associativity
(0.009\,s) and distributivity (0.006\,s) that previously required
26--71\,s of SAT solving. Notably, the Gr\"obner basis makes the
multiplier encoding and SAT solver choice \emph{irrelevant} for
polynomial equation benchmarks: all encoding$\times$solver
combinations produce the same $<$10\,ms result.

When the Gr\"obner basis returns inconclusive (non-polynomial
residual constraints), a candidate assignment is extracted from the
reduced basis and used to generate assumption-gated SAT hints that
guide the bit-blasting solver. Problems with non-polynomial
operations (bitwise XOR, shifts, inequalities) fall through to
bit-blasting with \combacs. The implementation uses \cbmc's existing
\texttt{mp\_integer} for coefficient arithmetic, with no external
dependencies ($\sim$700 lines). The Gr\"obner basis computation is
a standalone decision procedure for the polynomial fragment of
QF\_BV (equations involving addition, multiplication, and
truncation); problems with bitwise operations, inequalities, or
shifts fall through to bit-blasting.

We measured the overhead of unsuccessful algebraic layers on
benchmarks that require SAT solving (bf16\_mul\_comm\_v2,
strength\_chain\_16, div\_mul\_roundtrip\_12). Word-level
simplification adds $<$0.02\,s, and the vanishing polynomial test
plus Gr\"obner basis add $<$0.05\,s---less than 0.5\% of the
hardest benchmark's solving time. The layers are effectively free
when they do not apply.

\section{Vanishing Polynomial Test}
\label{sec:vanishing}

Ideal membership in $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is not the
question an SMT user is asking. A verification engineer querying
$\forall a, b. \, f(a, b) = g(a, b)$ over 16-bit integers means
``for all 16-bit values of $a$ and $b$'' --- they want
\emph{function equivalence} on the bit-vector domain. Over a field,
the two coincide; over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ they do not. Some
polynomials are non-zero in $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}[x]$ but vanish as
functions on $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$: the canonical example is
$4x^2 + 4x \equiv 0 \pmod{8}$ for all
$x \in \{0, 1, \ldots, 7\}$, even though the polynomial has
non-zero coefficients. We refer to such polynomials as
\emph{vanishing polynomials}~\cite{shekhar2007equivalence}; they
exist because $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}[x]$ has more polynomials than
there are distinct $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$-valued functions on
$\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$.

The Gr\"obner-basis-only treatment of
Song et al.~\cite{song2024equational} decides the equational
theory (polynomial identity in $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}[x_1, \ldots,
x_n]$), not the functional theory (equivalence as functions on
$\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}^n \to \mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$). For SMT queries the
gap matters: it surfaces in DSP datapath verification where
coefficient scaling causes overflow cancellation, and the two
sides of an equivalence query differ as polynomials but agree as
functions.

This section gives a decision procedure for function equivalence
over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n_1}} \times \cdots \times
\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n_k}} \to \mathbb{Z}_{2^m}$, complete in that
sense. We adapt the algebraic test of
Shekhar et al.~\cite{shekhar2007equivalence}, with the efficient
falling-factorial / Stirling-number / Kronecker-product
normalisation algorithm of
G\`amez-Montolio et al.~\cite{gamez2024efficient}.

\paragraph{Why a separate test, instead of adding vanishing
polynomials to the Gr\"obner basis?}
A natural alternative is to add the generators of the vanishing
ideal --- the polynomials
$2^{\max(d - \nu_2(k!),\ 0)} \cdot x^{\underline{k}}$ for
$k = 2, \ldots, \mathrm{SF}(2^d)$, per input variable --- directly
to the input basis of Buchberger's algorithm. The resulting strong
Gr\"obner basis would automatically reduce vanishing polynomials to
zero, so ideal membership would coincide with functional
equivalence and a separate test would be unnecessary.

We implemented this experimentally and found it impractical. On
the full 39-benchmark custom suite, replacing the falling-factorial
test with vanishing-polynomial injection \emph{loses} 9 of 39
benchmarks --- including 7
(\texttt{overflow\_detect\_16}, \texttt{add\_overflow\_16},
\texttt{checked\_mul\_16}, \texttt{equiv\_unsat\_8add\_16},
\texttt{crypto\_square\_mod}, \texttt{mul\_ineq\_12},
\texttt{fixedpoint\_mul\_16}) that do not need vanishing reasoning
at all and solve trivially without the test. Adding the injection
on \emph{top} of the working vanishing test causes 2 further
regressions
(\texttt{div\_mul\_roundtrip\_12}: $3.97\,$s $\to$ T/O,
\texttt{fixedpoint\_mul\_16}: $0.02\,$s $\to$ T/O). Two failure modes:
(i) For $d = 8$, $\mathrm{SF}(2^d) = 10$, so 9 generators per
variable are added; their pairwise S-polynomials bloat
Buchberger's worklist even when not needed, hitting the
100\,000-step reduction budget.
(ii) The DSP benchmarks introduce intermediate variables for
nested multiplications via SSA (\texttt{x2 = x*x}, \texttt{x3 =
x*x2}, $\ldots$, up to \texttt{x10}). To use a degree-10 vanishing
polynomial in $x$, Buchberger must first reduce the polynomial
back to a polynomial in $x$ alone, an S-polynomial chain that
exceeds the step budget. The falling-factorial test sidesteps
both issues by substituting SSA definitions before testing and
converting directly to the factorial basis. Full data in the
supplementary material.

\paragraph{Direction of completeness.}
The vanishing-polynomial test, and the Gr\"obner-basis solver
of Section~\ref{sec:algebraic} more broadly, decide UNSAT-direction
queries: they prove that two polynomials are equal as functions
(equivalently, that a disequality is unsatisfiable) but cannot
construct a witness when they differ. SAT-finding queries
(``does $p(x) = c$ have a solution?'', root-finding, factoring)
require the bit-blast layer instead. Section~\ref{sec:eval-random-poly}
quantifies this: on random polynomial-identity benchmarks our
algebraic procedure dominates on UNSAT-oriented categories
(addition / multiplication / encoding-correctness identities) but
loses to bit-blasting on equality and nonequality SAT-finding
queries.

\subsection{Falling Factorials and Stirling Numbers}
\label{sec:vanishing-falling}

Let $R = \mathbb{Z}_{2^m}$ and consider $R$-valued functions on
$k$ bit-vector inputs of widths $n_1, \ldots, n_k$. For a natural
number $j$, the \emph{falling factorial} is
\[
  x^{\underline{j}} \;=\; x \cdot (x{-}1) \cdot (x{-}2) \cdots (x{-}j{+}1),
\]
with $x^{\underline{0}} = 1$. The polynomials $\{x^{\underline{j}}\}_{j \geq 0}$
form a basis of $R[x]$ alternative to the monomial basis
$\{x^j\}_{j \geq 0}$.

\emph{Stirling numbers of the second kind}, $S(n,k)$, encode the
change of basis from monomials to falling factorials:
\[
  x^n \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{n} S(n,k) \, x^{\underline{k}},
  \qquad S(n,k) \;=\; \frac{1}{k!} \sum_{j=0}^{k} (-1)^{k-j}
                       \binom{k}{j} j^{\,n}.
\]
Combinatorially, $S(n,k)$ counts the number of ways to partition
an $n$-element set into $k$ non-empty blocks. For bit-vector
verification, the key property is that in $R = \mathbb{Z}_{2^m}$,
the falling factorial $x^{\underline{j}}$ is divisible by $j!$ as a
function of $x$ (since any consecutive $j$ integers contain a
multiple of each prime power up to the $j!$ factor). Hence the
function $x^{\underline{j}} \bmod 2^m$ is identically zero whenever
$\nu_2(j!) \geq m$, where $\nu_2$ is the 2-adic valuation.

\begin{theorem}[Vanishing criterion~\cite{shekhar2007equivalence}]
\label{thm:vanishing}
A univariate polynomial $p(x) = \sum_j c_j \, x^{\underline{j}} \in R[x]$
vanishes as a function on $\mathbb{Z}_{2^n}$ if and only if for each
$j$,
\[
  2^{\,m - \min(\nu_2(j!),\,m)} \;\big|\; c_j \quad\text{in }\mathbb{Z}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Multivariate Extension via Kronecker Product}
\label{sec:vanishing-kron}

For a multivariate polynomial $p(x_1, \ldots, x_k) \in R[x_1, \ldots, x_k]$
we apply the change of basis independently per variable. Let
$S_{n_i}$ denote the $n_i \times n_i$ Stirling matrix of the second
kind. The change-of-basis matrix for the tensor product
$R[x_1] \otimes \cdots \otimes R[x_k]$ is the Kronecker product
$S_{n_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes S_{n_k}$, mapping the coefficient
vector in the monomial basis to the coefficient vector in the
multivariate falling-factorial basis $\{x_1^{\underline{j_1}} \cdots
x_k^{\underline{j_k}}\}$.

The vanishing criterion extends: $p$ is identically zero as a
function iff every multivariate falling-factorial coefficient
$c_{j_1, \ldots, j_k}$ satisfies the divisibility
$2^{m - \min(\nu_2(\prod_i j_i!), m)} \mid c_{j_1, \ldots, j_k}$.

\paragraph{Mixed input widths.}
Variables in the same polynomial ring $R[x_1, \ldots, x_k]$ may have
different effective input widths $n_i \leq d$ (e.g., when narrower
operands are zero-extended into a wider product). The criterion
above is incomplete for that setting: the falling factorial
$x_i^{\underline{k}}$ is identically zero on
$\{0, 1, \ldots, 2^{n_i} - 1\}$ once $k \geq 2^{n_i}$, so terms
exceeding that bound vanish on the input range without contributing
a divisibility constraint. Our implementation tracks the input width
of each variable through the polynomial extractor and adjusts the
per-variable degree bound to $\mu_i = \min(2^{n_i}, \mathrm{SF}(2^d))$.
Two of our DSP benchmarks exercise this mixed-width form:
\texttt{dsp\_image\_reject}
($\mathbb{Z}_{2^{12}} \times \mathbb{Z}_{2^8} \to \mathbb{Z}_{2^{16}}$,
solved in 14\,ms) and \texttt{dsp\_vanishing\_mv}
($\mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_{2^2} \to \mathbb{Z}_{2^3}$,
solved in 5\,ms).

\subsection{Algorithm and Complexity}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Convert the difference polynomial to the falling factorial
  basis using Stirling numbers of the second kind $S(n,k)$
  (Section~\ref{sec:vanishing-falling}).
\item For multivariate polynomials, use the Kronecker product
  $S^{\otimes k}$ for the basis change (Section~\ref{sec:vanishing-kron}).
\item Check the divisibility conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:vanishing}.
\item If all conditions hold, the polynomial vanishes.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Complexity derivation.} Let $d_w$ denote the maximum
per-variable degree of the difference polynomial (in the monomial
basis), and $k$ the number of variables. The monomial basis has
$d_w^k$ elements; the Kronecker Stirling matrix has
$d_w^k \times d_w^k$ entries. The basis change is a single
matrix-vector product, giving cost $O(d_w^{2k})$. For the typical
DSP datapaths we verify, $d_w \leq 4$ and $k \leq 4$, so the matrix
has at most $4^4 = 256$ rows; the test completes in microseconds.
For higher-degree benchmarks the exponential dependence on $k$ is
real but bounded in practice by the polynomial structure of DSP
filters (which rarely exceed degree 3 per variable and 4 variables).
The test is complete for polynomial equivalence over
$\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n_1}} \times \cdots \times \mathbb{Z}_{2^{n_d}}
\to \mathbb{Z}_{2^m}$.

\subsection{Worked Example: $4x^2 + 4x \equiv 0 \pmod{8}$}
\label{sec:vanishing-example}

To see the mechanism, take $R = \mathbb{Z}_8$, $m = 3$, and
$p(x) = 4x^2 + 4x$ as a univariate polynomial.
Convert to falling factorials using $x^2 = x^{\underline{2}} + x^{\underline{1}}$
and $x = x^{\underline{1}}$:
\[
  p(x) \;=\; 4(x^{\underline{2}} + x^{\underline{1}}) + 4 x^{\underline{1}}
        \;=\; 4 \, x^{\underline{2}} + 8 \, x^{\underline{1}}.
\]
Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:vanishing}. For $c_2 = 4$: $\nu_2(2!) = 1$,
so we need $2^{3-1} = 4 \mid 4$, which holds. For $c_1 = 8$:
$\nu_2(1!) = 0$, so we need $2^3 = 8 \mid 8$, which also holds.
All conditions hold, so $p$ vanishes as a function---which matches
the direct verification $4 \cdot 0 + 4 \cdot 0 = 0$, $4 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 1 = 8 \equiv 0$,
$4 \cdot 4 + 4 \cdot 2 = 24 \equiv 0$, and so on.

In the DSP context this is important because a Horner-form filter
and its expanded form typically differ by terms of this shape
(low-order bits cancel through overflow).

\subsection{How Overflow Detection Becomes a Polynomial Query}
\label{sec:overflow-encoding}

Readers may wonder how overflow-detection benchmarks become amenable
to polynomial reasoning, since ``$x$ overflows $N$ bits'' is
quintessentially a non-polynomial bit-level predicate. The mechanism
is structural: overflow-detection benchmarks typically assert a
\emph{disequality} between two values that, modulo $2^N$, are
necessarily equal --- so the disequality reduces to a polynomial
identity in $\mathbb{Z}_{2^N}$ regardless of whether overflow occurs.

Consider \texttt{overflow\_detect\_16}:
\begin{verbatim}
(define-fun wide_prod   () (_ BitVec 32) (bvmul wa wb))
(define-fun narrow_prod () (_ BitVec 16) (bvmul a b))
(assert (not (= narrow_prod ((_ extract 15 0) wide_prod))))
\end{verbatim}
The assertion is unsatisfiable not because of any reasoning about
the high 16 bits of \texttt{wide\_prod}, but because
$\texttt{narrow\_prod} = a\cdot b \bmod 2^{16}$ and
$\texttt{extract}(15,0)(\texttt{wide\_prod})
= (\texttt{zero\_extend}(a) \cdot \texttt{zero\_extend}(b)) \bmod 2^{16}
= a\cdot b \bmod 2^{16}$. The Gr\"obner basis recognises the polynomial
identity in $\mathbb{Z}_{2^{16}}$. The ``overflow'' part of the query
acts as an unconstrained assumption that the algebraic layer accepts
without analysing.

This is the typical encoding: an overflow predicate is paired with a
disequality whose two sides reduce to the same polynomial in the
narrow ring. Where an overflow predicate appears as the \emph{primary}
constraint --- e.g.\ ``prove that $a\cdot b$ overflows iff
$a > 2^N / b$'' --- our extractor cannot turn the high-bit extraction
\texttt{(extract $2N{-}1$ $N$)} into a polynomial directly (the
non-zero low index is a shift, which is non-polynomial); the query
falls through to the bit-blast layer. Such cases (e.g.\ the
no-overflow assumptions in \texttt{mul\_ineq\_12}) still benefit
indirectly: the Gr\"obner basis simplifies the multiplication-related
sub-queries, leaving the bit-blast layer with a smaller search.

\subsection{Worked Example: Gr\"obner Basis for Commutativity at BW=3}
\label{sec:grobner-example}

Consider $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$ over $\mathbb{Z}_8$ ($d = 3$).
The Rabinowitsch-augmented polynomial system is
\[
  \{\; p_1 = z_1 - a \cdot b, \quad
       p_2 = z_2 - b \cdot a, \quad
       p_3 = (z_1 - z_2) \cdot e - 1 \;\},
\]
with lex order $e > z_1 > z_2 > a > b$.

Step 1. Reduce $p_3$ by $p_1$: substitute $z_1 \mapsto a \cdot b$
(modulo the sign of the S-polynomial), giving
$p_3' = (a \cdot b - z_2) \cdot e - 1$.

Step 2. Reduce $p_3'$ by $p_2$: substitute $z_2 \mapsto b \cdot a$,
giving $p_3'' = (a \cdot b - b \cdot a) \cdot e - 1$.

Step 3. In $\mathbb{Z}_8[a, b, e]$, $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$ holds as
a polynomial identity (commutativity of the ring), so
$p_3'' = 0 \cdot e - 1 = -1$. The constant $-1 \equiv 7 \pmod{8}$ is
odd, hence a unit: the Gr\"obner basis contains a unit, so the
original system is unsatisfiable, proving $z_1 = z_2$ for all
assignments. This takes three reductions, regardless of $d$. In
our implementation the cost is ${\sim}$200\,$\mu$s independent of
bitwidth.

\subsection{Amulet2 Comparison}
\label{sec:eval-amulet}

Kaufmann and Biere's Amulet2~\cite{kaufmann2021amulet} verifies
gate-level multiplier circuits using Gr\"obner bases over
$\mathbb{Z}$ (with field polynomials $x^2 - x = 0$ to model
Booleans). Their tool works at the AIG level, not at the
SMT-LIB level; the natural comparison is scope rather than
head-to-head time.

Amulet verifies a specific multiplier implementation (the AIG
circuit it is given) against the integer multiplication
specification; we verify an SMT query involving one or more
uninterpreted \texttt{bvmul} calls. The two use cases overlap when
a verification problem can be phrased either way.

We ran Amulet2.1 (compiled from source, default configuration,
300\,s timeout) on the four AIG multiplier benchmarks shipped with
its artifact (\texttt{u\_4bp\_wt\_lf} at 128, 256, 512, 1024 bits;
Booth partial-product generator, Wallace-tree accumulation,
Ladner-Fischer final adder). Amulet times out on all four
benchmarks within the 300\,s budget. These are the larger
scalability benchmarks in the distribution; Amulet's own published
evaluation~\cite{kaufmann2021amulet} uses 64-bit multipliers
which verify in fractions of a second (${<}$1\,s per benchmark
according to the artifact's timing data). At 64 bits the AIG
structure is small enough that gate-level algebraic reasoning
works well; at 128+ bits the field-polynomial approach accumulates
symbolic overhead that the 300\,s budget cannot absorb.

Our Gr\"obner-basis solver, at an equivalent-sized problem (SMT
query $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$ at BW$=$256), reports the identity in
$<$5\,ms (Table~\ref{tab:scaling}). The two numbers are not
directly comparable: Amulet verifies a specific AIG
implementation, while we verify the abstract identity $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$
against itself, which our solver recognises algebraically. Amulet
would also handle this case in a few milliseconds at its natural
64-bit scale; beyond that, the field-polynomial cost dominates.

The difference in coefficient rings matters. Amulet uses
$\mathbb{Q}$ with field polynomials $x^2 - x$, which makes every
bit a field element but produces exponential polynomial blowup in
the worst case. We use $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ with no field polynomials,
which avoids the blowup but loses field structure (division only
works for odd elements). This paper's empirical finding is that on
arithmetic-identity queries the $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ approach is
dramatically faster because the unit-constant check terminates
quickly; on gate-level circuit verification we expect Amulet to
retain its advantage at modest bitwidths due to the completeness
of its field-based reduction.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\textbf{Benchmark selection.} The 39-benchmark custom suite is
biased toward multiplication-heavy arithmetic queries common in
cryptography and DSP but rare in typical pointer-safety checking.
We include the 66-benchmark SMT-COMP sample
(Section~\ref{sec:eval-smtcomp}) and the 26-benchmark SMT-LIB
community set (Section~\ref{sec:eval-community}) explicitly to
counter this bias. The community sets are where our approach
looks most neutral (matching shift-add on SMT-COMP sample; losing
to Bitwuzla by one benchmark on SMT-LIB set).

\textbf{DSP benchmark design.} The 5 DSP datapath benchmarks in
the custom suite were designed by us to exercise the
vanishing-polynomial test; their inclusion shows the test works
as intended but should not be read as evidence of general
superiority. Our finding that all three tools solve these in
$<$50\,ms is therefore best interpreted as validating that the
test is correctly implemented rather than demonstrating a practical
advantage.

\textbf{Higher bitwidths.} The Gr\"obner-basis procedure scales
independently of bitwidth in principle
(Section~\ref{sec:scaling}). The community benchmarks include
up to 512-bit operands which our implementation does not yet
handle on every instance; understanding the remaining gap is
future work.

\textbf{Gate-level equivalence is a gap.} Our procedure is
complete for the polynomial ideal-membership fragment, but
\emph{gate-level} multiplier equivalence checking---the problem
Amulet2 (Section~\ref{sec:eval-amulet}) solves---sits just outside
our coverage. A natural way to encode a gate-level circuit in our
setting is to add a field polynomial $x \cdot (x - 1) = 0$ for
every bit variable, forcing it to be Boolean, and then to check
that the circuit's output polynomial equals the specification. We
tested this on a small hardware-multiplier-equivalence benchmark
(\texttt{hw\_mul\_equiv}): at BW=2 the augmented system has
a strong Gr\"obner basis of 23 elements including a unit constant,
so we correctly report UNSAT. At BW$\geq$4, however, the basis
converges to 18 elements with no unit constant, and our procedure
returns ``unknown.'' This is consistent with the literature: the
gate-level approaches of
Biere, Kauers, and Ritirc~\cite{biere2017challenges} and
Kaufmann and Biere~\cite{kaufmann2021amulet} exploit the topology
of the AIG (reverse topological ordering of gate polynomials) to
avoid computing a full Gr\"obner basis in the first place; a
general-purpose Buchberger loop cannot reproduce that structural
shortcut without explicit circuit information. Bridging this gap
is a promising direction for future work (one could, for example,
extract and use the SSA topological order of \cbmc's bit-level
decomposition).

\textbf{No correctness regressions.} The 691 \cbmc regression
tests (covering array bounds, pointer safety, floating-point, and
other properties) all pass with the algebraic layers enabled. The
algebraic layers add $<$4\,ms of overhead on benchmarks that do
not exercise them.

\textbf{Interaction with bit-blasting.} This paper shares with the
bit-blasting companion paper~\cite{companion-bitblasting} the
observation that \cadical's
inprocessing BVE can be counterproductive for carry-save popcount
encodings (4--140\% overhead in one subset of benchmarks). In
practice the adaptive heuristic in the bit-blasting solver routes
such multiplications to shift-add when BVE would cascade
usefully.


\section{Mechanised Soundness Proofs}
\label{sec:lean4}

We verified the soundness of the Gr\"obner basis solver with 25
theorems in Lean~4 (Lean 4.18.0, Mathlib v4.18.0), covering:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Unit characterisation in $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$}: odd
  elements are units (coprime to $2^d$); 2 is not a unit for
  $d \geq 1$. (Submitted to Mathlib as PR \#38628.)
\item \textbf{Ideal preservation}: S-polynomial computation and
  polynomial reduction preserve ideal membership.
\item \textbf{Rabinowitsch soundness}: unit in ideal $\Rightarrow$
  no solution, via ring homomorphism to a nontrivial ring.
\item \textbf{Buchberger termination}: ascending chain condition
  in Noetherian rings guarantees termination.
\end{enumerate}

All proofs compile with zero \texttt{sorry}.


\section{Related Work}

We organise related work by which corner of the four-classes
taxonomy (Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy}) each line of work attacks.

\paragraph{Universal-equational, ideal membership.}
Song et al.~\cite{song2024equational} prove that strong Gr\"obner
bases over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ solve the equational theory of
bit-vectors --- the theoretical foundation we build on.
Bryant~\cite{bryant1991complexity} earlier showed that BDD-based
multiplication verification requires exponential size, motivating
non-BDD algebraic approaches. Clegg
et al.~\cite{clegg1996groebner} proved the Gr\"obner proof system
can be exponentially more powerful than resolution, giving the
proof-theoretic justification for algebraic methods on
multiplication. Our contribution beyond Song et al.\ is the SMT
integration: SSA-aware decomposition of nested multiplications,
the Rabinowitsch trick for disequalities, progress-based
termination, and mechanised soundness
(Section~\ref{sec:algebraic-contrib}).

\paragraph{Universal-equational, function equivalence.}
Shekhar et al.~\cite{shekhar2007equivalence} characterise the
vanishing ideal of $\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n_1}} \times \cdots \times
\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n_d}} \to \mathbb{Z}_{2^m}$ using falling
factorials and give a complete decision procedure for polynomial
equivalence in this setting; G\`amez-Montolio
et al.~\cite{gamez2024efficient} give the efficient normalisation
algorithm (via Stirling numbers and Kronecker products) that we
implement. Howe et al.~\cite{howe2025subpolynomial} decompose
univariate polynomials into subpolynomials for compact
bit-blasting; the technique is orthogonal to our
vanishing-polynomial test but could combine with it on queries
that fall through to the bit-blast layer.

\paragraph{Universal-equational on gate-level circuits.}
Gate-level algebraic verification is a related but distinct line:
the input is an AIG circuit, not an SMT query. Biere, Kauers, and
Ritirc~\cite{biere2017challenges} observed that gate polynomials
form a Gr\"obner basis under reverse topological ordering,
yielding polynomial-time multiplier verification.
Kaufmann~\cite{kaufmann2020thesis} and Kaufmann and
Biere~\cite{kaufmann2021amulet} extended this to the Amulet2 tool
with XOR slicing and proof certificates (PAC/LPAC).
Yu et al.~\cite{yu2016arithmetic} achieve linear-time gate-level
verification via function extraction. These approaches work over
$\mathbb{Q}$ with field polynomials $x^2 - x = 0$ to model
Booleans; ours works directly over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$, avoiding
the exponential polynomial blowup field polynomials cause but
losing field structure. Section~\ref{sec:eval-amulet} compares
in detail.

\paragraph{Existential-equational and finite-field
generalisations.}
\v{O}zdemir et al.~\cite{Ozdemir23cvc5}
integrate CoCoALib~\cite{abbott:cocoalib} into cvc5 for
Gr\"obner-basis reasoning over prime-order finite fields,
targeting QF\_FF queries (e.g., zkSNARK constraint systems);
their procedure handles both UNSAT and SAT directions via root
finding. The setting is different: prime-order fields admit
field structure and complete univariate root finding, while
$\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ does not. The two procedures are complementary
on cryptographic verification: finite-field methods excel on
curve-based cryptography, our approach on bit-vector arithmetic
identities. The cvc5 \texttt{--ff} flag activates this only for
QF\_FF logic; QF\_BV queries do not invoke it.

\paragraph{Heuristic word-level rewriting (covers all four
classes).}
Niemetz et al.~\cite{niemetz2023bitwuzla} implement occurrence-map
canonicalisation in Bitwuzla that handles commutativity and
associativity via syntactic rewriting; cvc5's QF\_BV pipeline
applies pattern-matched rewrites and a Gaussian-elimination
preprocessor for linear systems modulo $2^d$. Both approaches are
\emph{heuristic} in our sense: they decide the queries that match
their pattern set or admit a linear reduction, and fall through
to bit-blasting on queries that escape. They cover all four
taxonomy classes when the rewriter has matching patterns; on
queries with high-degree polynomial structure or vanishing
polynomials they fall through. Our procedure is complementary:
on the universal-equational class with polynomial expressions in
$\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$, it is a decision procedure rather than a
heuristic; on the other three classes it falls through to
bit-blasting in turn.

\paragraph{Relational classes.}
The bit-blasting companion paper~\cite{companion-bitblasting}
addresses the universal-relational and existential-relational
classes more uniformly than our procedure does. Its
multiplier-encoding contributions are orthogonal to the present
work: the algebraic procedure decides queries that escape any
multiplier encoding, while the bit-blasting companion paper
improves the encoding for queries that reach the SAT solver.

\paragraph{Proof complexity.}
The extended Frege proof system (including Gr\"obner-basis
reasoning) has polynomial proofs of arithmetic identities such
as commutativity~\cite{buss1995bounded}, a fact we rely on
implicitly: our algebraic procedure can be viewed as constructing
such a proof. Whether resolution also has polynomial proofs of
these identities is open~\cite{biere2017challenges}; the
bit-blasting companion paper~\cite{companion-bitblasting}
provides empirical evidence that it does not (integer
commutativity is $1000\times$ harder than GF(2) in CDCL).


\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

We presented two algebraic techniques for bit-vector arithmetic
verification: a strong Gr\"obner basis solver over
$\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ for polynomial ideal membership, and a
vanishing polynomial test for DSP datapath equivalence. Empirical
evaluation across three benchmark pools (Section~\ref{sec:external})
shows the techniques to be a complementary fast path for arithmetic
identities --- matching mature word-level reasoners on simple
identities, dispatching high-degree polynomial-identity benchmarks
that bit-blasting alone cannot handle, and adding negligible
overhead on queries they do not solve. They do not replace
word-level reasoning on general QF\_BV benchmarks, where
syntactic methods remain stronger.

Several directions remain open. First, the Gr\"obner basis is
incomplete for non-polynomial constraints; theory combination could
close this gap. Second, post-quantum cryptographic
implementations (e.g., SABER) use polynomial ring multiplication
that our techniques could verify.

\paragraph{Towards a true theory solver for bit-vector arithmetic.}
Our current architecture treats the algebraic layer as a
preprocessor: it runs once, decides what it can, and the residual
goes to bit-blasting. A natural next step is to make the layer a
full theory solver in the SMT(LRA/UF/$\ldots$)\ tradition,
exchanging information bidirectionally with the SAT search.
Two concrete sub-directions:
\emph{(a) Expression-level normalisation.} After Buchberger, reduce
arbitrary polynomial expressions in the residual w.r.t.\ the
computed basis. If the reduction produces a constant or a shorter
polynomial form, communicate it to the bit-blast layer as a
SAT-level equality, propagating the algebraic insight. This
generalises our existing \texttt{extract\_candidate} (which finds
univariate-linear basis elements only) to arbitrary expressions.
\emph{(b) Bit-level partial information as polynomial constraints.}
SAT-level partial assignments often determine specific bits of
intermediate values. Encoding these via fresh bit-decomposition
variables $b_{x,i}$ over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$, with idempotency
$b_{x,i}^2 = b_{x,i}$ and the sum-decomposition
$x = \sum_i 2^i b_{x,i}$, allows the algebraic layer to
incorporate bit-level facts and to discover bit-level consequences.
Together these would close the equation-vs-expression compositional
gap that Section~\ref{sec:algebraic} introduced.
