# Analysis of Martin's notes (2026-05-15)

This document analyses Martin's review feedback on Paper 2 (algebraic)
and identifies actionable items.

## Martin's points and our response

### 1. "How much do you / we care about the high word of a multiply / about expanding multiply (N*N→2N)?"

**Context**: Martin offers to create benchmarks for this form.

**Status**: Paper 2 §3.3 has `dsp_image_reject` (uint12 × uint8 → uint16)
that uses widening multiplication. The custom suite has
`overflow_detect_16` (uint16 × uint16 → uint32 with overflow check) and
`mul_ineq_12`. So the form is partially covered.

**Action**: would benefit from more benchmarks targeting:
- N×N → 2N expanding multiplication for various N (cryptographic
  primitives use this).
- High-word access patterns (`(extract (2N-1) N) (bvmul ...)`).
- Mixed widening / narrowing patterns common in hashing, fixed-point
  arithmetic.

These would extend Paper 2's evaluation pool by 5-10 benchmarks
exercising the high-word semantics.

### 2. "Arnau ran into uniqueness problems with Gröbner bases — unique up to what?"

**Context**: Strong Gröbner bases over Z_{2^d} (a ring with zero
divisors) don't have the same uniqueness properties as Gröbner bases
over a field. Different orderings or normal-form choices can yield
different bases.

**Status**: Paper 2 §2.1 mentions strong Gröbner bases but does not
explicitly discuss uniqueness. §2.4 (ordering ablation, Table on
line 238) shows orderings produce timings within noise but does not
discuss whether the *bases themselves* differ.

**Action**: §2.1 should briefly note that strong Gröbner bases over
Z_{2^d} are unique up to leading-coefficient unit multiplication
when normalized (the standard fact for principal ideal rings in
each leading-coefficient orbit). The ordering ablation supports
this empirically.

### 3. "Song et al. probably don't know about zero-function polynomials (ZFPs)"

**Context**: ZFPs are polynomials non-zero as polynomials but zero as
functions over Z_{2^d}. Example: `4x² + 4x ≡ 0 (mod 8)`.

**Status**: Paper 2 §3 IS the vanishing polynomial test, which is
exactly the ZFP-handling extension. We use Stirling-number /
falling-factorial decomposition (Shekhar-Kalla 2007 + Gámez-Montolio
et al. 2024 Algorithm 3).

**Action**: §3 already addresses this. The framing could be sharper —
explicitly call out that this is the "ZFP gap" missed by basic
Gröbner-over-Z_{2^d} approaches like Song et al.

### 4. "Add ZFPs directly to the Gröbner basis"

**Context**: Martin's "less stupid" idea: instead of running Gröbner
*then* the vanishing polynomial test as separate phases, add ZFP
generators to the basis at the start. Buchberger then automatically
reduces ZFPs to zero, and ideal membership is the same as functional
equivalence.

**Status**: Paper 2 currently keeps these as two phases. The vanishing
test (§3) runs as a separate complete-equivalence check.

**Action**: This is a substantial research direction. Concretely:
- Compute generators of the ZFP ideal `Z_n,d` for Z_{2^d}[x_1, ..., x_n].
- For univariate Z_{2^d}[x]: the ZFP ideal is generated by polynomials
  of the form `2^{d-ν₂(k!)} · k! · binom(x, k) = 2^{d-ν₂(k!)} · (x)_k`
  (falling factorials of suitable degree).
- For multivariate: tensor-product structure (each variable contributes
  its univariate ZFP generators).
- Add these to the input basis, run Buchberger as usual.

If this works, §3 simplifies dramatically: the vanishing test is
absorbed into the Gröbner basis computation. The basis grows by ~d
extra generators per variable, but Buchberger should converge faster
because ZFP reductions are now first-class moves.

**Empirical test**: implement ZFP injection, re-run Paper 2's §3.3 DSP
benchmarks. If they still solve in <15 ms, the simplification is a
clean replacement. If timings degrade, document the tradeoff.

### 5. "Overflow detection encoding — I don't immediately see it."

**Context**: Martin asks how overflow detection reduces to a
polynomial query.

**Status**: Paper 2 doesn't explicitly walk through this. Inspecting
`overflow_detect_16.smt2`:

```smt2
(define-fun wide_prod () (_ BitVec 32) (bvmul wa wb))   ; 32-bit
(define-fun narrow_prod () (_ BitVec 16) (bvmul a b))   ; 16-bit
(assert (not (= narrow_prod ((_ extract 15 0) wide_prod))))
```

The assertion `narrow_prod ≠ extract(15,0)(wide_prod)` is unsat
*regardless of overflow* because `narrow_prod = a·b mod 2^16` and
`extract(15,0)(wide_prod) = (zero_extend(a)·zero_extend(b)) mod 2^16
= a·b mod 2^16`. The polynomial identity reduces in Z_{2^16}.

The "overflow" assertion (`extract(31,16)(wide_prod) ≠ 0`) is
*irrelevant* to the proof — it's an ASSUMPTION the algebraic solver
accepts unconstrained, since the disequality alone is unsat.

**Action**: §3 (or wherever overflow_detect is discussed) should add
a paragraph explaining this mechanism. Otherwise readers wonder how
overflow becomes polynomial.

The mechanism for `mul_ineq_12` (multiplication monotonicity under
no-overflow) is more subtle: the no-overflow assumptions are
`extract(23,12)(bvmul wa wc) = 0`, which our extractor cannot
directly turn into a polynomial (high-bit extract is non-zero index).
Verification falls through to the bit-blast layer, which leverages
the assumptions to constrain the search. This is documented less
clearly in Paper 2.

### 6. "Reduce all polynomials, including expressions, using the Gröbner basis."

**Context**: Martin's "mildly less stupid" idea — use the Gröbner
basis as a normalizer for arbitrary polynomial expressions before
bit-blasting. Theory-solver style.

**Status**: Currently the algebraic layer either solves the entire
disequality or falls through to bit-blasting. There's no
intermediate normalization step.

**Action**: research direction. Implementation sketch:
- After the Gröbner basis is computed (whether or not it solves the
  query), keep it.
- For each remaining `mult_exprt` that goes to bit-blasting, compute
  its polynomial form and reduce w.r.t. the basis. If the reduced form
  is simpler (e.g., a constant or a single variable), substitute.
- This propagates algebraic insight into bit-blasted regions.

Compositionally interesting; would benefit from Arnau's interpolation
work (point 8 below).

### 7. "Equations vs expressions"

**Context**: Martin's pithy framing: "Gröbner bases work with equations,
bit-blasting works with expressions."

**Status**: Paper 2 acknowledges this implicitly — the algebraic layer
collects equations (SSA equalities and disequalities) and the
bit-blast layer handles expression conversion separately.

**Action**: useful framing for §1 (introduction) or §5 (discussion).
Makes the architectural choice explicit: Paper 2's contribution is to
add an *equation-level* solver alongside CBMC's existing
*expression-level* layer.

### 8. "Interpolation (Arnau's work)"

**Context**: If we have multivariate polynomial p(x,y) with constraints
on x, y, and p(x,y), can we use these to constrain interpolations of
p(x,_) and p(_,y)?

**Status**: Out of scope for Paper 2 as currently structured. Arnau's
interpolation algorithms are a separate strand.

**Action**: future work / discussion of related techniques. Worth a
sentence in §6 (related work) noting that interpolation-based methods
could compose with our algebraic layer.

### 9. "Bit-level partial information as polynomials"

**Context**: Martin's idea — represent partial information (3rd bit of
x is 1, 7th bit of 3x⁷-5y is 0) as polynomial constraints. Do actual
theory solver stuff.

**Status**: Out of scope. CBMC's existing `extractbits` mechanism is
the standard way to get bit-level information; encoding it
polynomially would require either (a) introducing 2^d extra variables
per bit (one per bit position, with `b_i ∈ {0, 1}` constraints) or
(b) a more elaborate bit-level polynomial encoding.

**Action**: future work. Mention in discussion as a way to extend the
algebraic layer's reach into bit-level reasoning.

   Paper 2's contribution is an equation-level solver alongside the

### N×N→2N benchmarks (half a day)

   patterns (mulhi, double-width modular products), high-word access
   patterns. Add to Paper 2's evaluation pool. Re-run the wider
   five-approach comparison on these.

   **Martin has already generated benchmarks** at
   <https://github.com/martin-cs/subpolynomial-encoding/tree/main/benchmarks>.
   Cloned to `/tmp/subpolynomial-encoding`. Two seed archives
   (`seed-23.tar.xz`, `seed-42.tar.xz`) with ~2,500 benchmarks each.
   Pattern: random degree-10 polynomials over Z_{2^16} testing
   `p(x) + q(x) = (p+q)(x)`, bounding, correctness, etc., in two

   - `addition` (polynomial sum identity)
   - `bounding` (degree bounds)

   ourselves; using them gives Paper 2 a broader empirical case.
