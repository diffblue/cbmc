\begin{abstract}
We present a methodology for designing SAT encodings of integer
multiplication guided by proof trace analysis. By examining DRAT
proofs, we identify structural bottlenecks---the top bottleneck
variable in standard Comba encoding appears in 31\% of all proof
steps---and use these findings to redesign the encoding. This
methodology produced \combacs, a carry-save Comba encoding that
separates column reduction from carry propagation, achieving
2.6--71$\times$ speedup across four SAT solvers. We further show
that carry propagation is the dominant driver of CDCL search
difficulty for multiplication on standard encodings---even though
polynomial-size resolution refutations of commutativity provably
exist~\cite{beame2019toward}. A controlled experiment holds the
partial-product layout and accumulation
topology fixed and varies only the accumulator (integer addition
vs.\ GF(2) XOR): on CaDiCaL, integer commutativity is at least
$1000\times$ harder than GF(2) at bit-width 12, and structural
variations (sequential vs.\ parallel-tree accumulation) change
running time by less than $5\times$. The gap is between
\emph{existence} of short proofs and the ability of CDCL
search to find them; we address it by engineering encodings
that guide CDCL toward those proofs. Comparing \combacs with pre-existing alternative
encodings (Booth radix-4, 4-bit blocks, sorting networks), we
find that no single encoding dominates: Booth is 17$\times$ faster
on strength-reduction benchmarks, while \combacs is 2$\times$
faster on bfloat16 commutativity. The optimal encoding depends on
problem structure, motivating adaptive selection.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Consider verifying that 16-bit multiplication commutes: does
$a \times b = b \times a$ hold for all 16-bit inputs $a$ and $b$?
A SAT-based bit-vector SMT solver encodes this as a CNF formula
with two multiplier circuits and an equality check. With the
standard shift-and-add encoding, \cadical~\cite{biere2019cadical}
times out after 120\,s. With our carry-save Comba encoding, it
solves in 5.6\,s---a 21$\times$ speedup from changing only the
encoding.\footnote{With \cbmc's algebraic pre-solver enabled (a
companion paper~\cite{companion-algebraic}), commutativity benchmarks are solved in
${\sim}$5\,ms regardless of encoding. The numbers here are with
the algebraic pre-solver disabled, isolating the encoding's
contribution. In the deployed configuration, the encoding matters
primarily for problems the algebraic layer cannot handle:
bitwise operations, inequalities, floating-point, and structural
mismatches.}

How did we arrive at this encoding? Not by theoretical analysis of
circuit complexity, but by a systematic methodology: generate a DRAT
proof of the existing encoding, identify which variables participate
most heavily in the proof, trace those variables back to the encoding
structure, and redesign to reduce the identified bottlenecks. This
proof-guided approach is transferable to other encoding problems and
is the primary contribution of this paper.

Bit-vector SMT solvers, including those used for bounded and
unbounded model checking, IC3-style invariant
inference~\cite{bradley2011sat}, hardware equivalence
checking, cryptographic circuit analysis, and symbolic execution,
reduce bit-vector formulas to SAT via \emph{bit-blasting}. For
formulas involving integer multiplication---common in cryptography,
DSP, overflow checking, and compiler verification---the encoding
choice dramatically affects solver performance. Yet the interaction
between encoding choices and modern
SAT solver techniques
(inprocessing~\cite{jarvisalo2012inprocessing},
BVE~\cite{een2005effective}) is poorly understood. Prior work has
focused on BDD-based complexity~\cite{bryant1991complexity} and
propagation completeness~\cite{brain2016propagation,brain2021wrong},
but not on systematic evaluation of SAT encodings across multiple
solvers with solver-level proof analysis.

Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Proof-guided encoding methodology.} We demonstrate a
  systematic approach: DRAT proof analysis $\to$ bottleneck
  identification $\to$ encoding redesign $\to$ validation. This
  produced \combacs and can be applied to other encoding problems
  (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).

\item \textbf{Carry propagation as the causal driver of CDCL
  search difficulty.} We combine a GF(2) comparison with a
  controlled five-variant experiment holding topology fixed and
  varying only the accumulator operator: on CaDiCaL, integer
  multiplication is orders of magnitude harder than GF(2) on
  structurally identical formulas at BW$\geq$8, while topology
  variations change running time by less than 5$\times$. This
  isolates the cause of the \emph{empirical} difficulty, rather
  than of proof-theoretic hardness---Beame and Liew have shown
  that polynomial-size regular resolution proofs of
  commutativity exist for array, diagonal, and Booth
  multipliers~\cite{beame2019toward}. The practical gap is
  that CDCL does not find those proofs without encoding-level
  guidance (Section~\ref{sec:hardness}).

\item \textbf{Six-encoding comparison with solver statistics.} We
  evaluate shift-add, Dadda, Comba, \combacs, Booth radix-4, 4-bit
  blocks, and sorting networks across four SAT solvers, reporting
  conflicts, propagations, BVE eliminations, fixed variables, and
  proof sizes. Results are presented with statistical variance,
  multi-layer ablation, and adaptive-heuristic ablation; no single
  encoding dominates
  (Sections~\ref{sec:combacs},~\ref{sec:alternative},~\ref{sec:evaluation}).

\item \textbf{Negative results.} We catalogue 20+ approaches that
  failed, including subquadratic algorithms, XOR Gaussian
  elimination, and abstraction refinement
  (Section~\ref{sec:negative}).
\end{enumerate}

Multiplication queries encountered by bit-vector SMT solvers fall
into two classes:
\emph{universal/equational} queries ($\forall x, y.\; P(x,y) =
Q(x,y)$, e.g., commutativity, hardware equivalence checking) and
\emph{existential} queries ($\exists x, y.\; P(x,y) = c$, e.g.,
factoring, overflow detection). Bit-blasting handles both but scales
poorly with bit-width; algebraic techniques (treated in a companion
paper~\cite{companion-algebraic}) handle universal queries
efficiently but are not constructive
for existential queries. This paper focuses on improving bit-blasting
for both classes. The same techniques apply to division and remainder
(encoding $a = b \cdot q + r$ with non-deterministic $q$, $r$
requires an internal multiplier).

\section{Background}

An $N$-bit unsigned multiplication $a \times b$ produces $N$ partial
products $\mathit{pp}[i] = a[i] \wedge b$, each shifted by $i$
positions. These must be accumulated into the final $2N$-bit product.
The standard \emph{shift-add} encoding adds partial products
sequentially using a ripple-carry adder chain, creating a carry chain
of length $O(N^2)$. \emph{Dadda trees}~\cite{dadda1965some} reduce
the partial-product grid to two rows using carry-save full adders,
followed by a single carry-propagate addition.
\emph{Comba}~\cite{comba1990exponentiation} reduces each column
independently using popcount (parallel bit counting via the pop0
algorithm from~\cite{warren2012hackers}), propagating carries to
adjacent columns during the same pass. Combining column-wise
popcount with immediate carry propagation couples two otherwise
independent sources of structure in the same set of clauses, which
limits how aggressively downstream CDCL inprocessing can
simplify the encoding. Section~\ref{sec:combacs} introduces our
carry-save Comba variant that decouples these two steps by first
reducing every column to a small set of sum/carry bits independently,
and only then propagating carries in a second pass.

Modern CDCL solvers employ techniques that interact with encoding
design in ways we exploit throughout this paper. Bounded Variable
Elimination (BVE)~\cite{een2005effective} eliminates variables by
resolving all clauses containing them; it is effective when the
resolvent count is small. \cadical~\cite{biere2019cadical} performs
BVE during search (\emph{inprocessing}), while
\minisat/SatELite~\cite{een2005effective} performs it only during
preprocessing. \cadical also extracts XOR and AND gates from CNF and
identifies congruent (functionally equivalent) gate pairs---for BW=9
commutativity, it finds 131 XOR gates, 138 AND gates, and 130
congruent pairs between two multiplier circuits.

Brain et al.~\cite{brain2016propagation} showed that composition of
propagation-complete (PC) primitives preserves PC for adders but
\emph{not} for multipliers. Brain~\cite{brain2021wrong} conjectured
that a PC multiplier encoding is exponential in size. Rather than
seeking propagation completeness, we optimise encoding structure for
interaction with BVE and inprocessing.

\section{Carry Propagation Hardness}
\label{sec:hardness}

We compare integer multiplication commutativity ($a \times b = b
\times a$) with GF(2) (carry-less) multiplication commutativity. Both
have identical partial-product grids; the only difference is carry
propagation (integer uses addition with carries, GF(2) uses XOR).
This comparison isolates carry propagation as a variable, though we
note that the change in addition operation also affects clause
structure and propagation properties.

At BW=13, integer multiplication needs 23$\times$ more conflicts than
GF(2) despite only 1.6$\times$ more clauses. Empirically, GF(2)
conflict count grows roughly as $n^3$ (measured across BW=5--17);
integer multiplication conflict count grows rapidly over the
bit-widths we tested. DRAT proof analysis
confirms: GF(2) proofs are 3.6--18$\times$ smaller.

\paragraph{The gap is between proof existence and CDCL search.}
It is important to be clear about what this data does and does
not mean. An influential line of work~\cite{beame2017towards,beame2019toward}
proves that ring identities such as commutativity and
distributivity on array, diagonal, and Booth multipliers admit
\emph{polynomial-size regular resolution refutations}---the very
proof system that CDCL solvers operate in. A slightly weaker
quasi-polynomial bound is known for Wallace tree multipliers.
The gap we observe is therefore not that short proofs do not
exist, but that current CDCL solvers do not find them on the
standard shift-add encoding. Beame and Liew themselves remark
on this gap and call it an open engineering problem: ``the
observed scaling of SAT solver performance on these problems
suggests that they do not currently find proofs matching even
these upper bounds. An important direction for improving SAT
solvers is to find the right guiding information to add, either
to the formulas derived from the circuits or to CDCL SAT solver
heuristics, to help them find shorter proofs''~\cite{beame2019toward}.
Our paper is squarely in the ``guiding information via encoding
structure'' direction: we engineer encodings (\combacs, Booth,
4-bit blocks) that let CDCL find the short proofs on instances
where the standard shift-add encoding does not.

\paragraph{From correlation to controlled experiment.}
The integer-vs-GF(2) comparison above changes three things
simultaneously: accumulator operator (add vs XOR), clause-level
structure, and variable dependencies. A reviewer can reasonably
ask which of these drives the difference. To isolate the
accumulator operator specifically, we constructed five benchmark
families in pure SMT-LIB with identical partial-product layouts
and identical accumulation topologies, changing only the
operator:
Each variant takes the $N$ partial products
$\mathit{pp}[i] = a[i] \wedge b$ (shifted by $i$) and differs
\emph{only} in how it sums them into the $2N$-bit output.
Let $\mathit{pp}_0, \ldots, \mathit{pp}_{N-1}$ be the shifted
partial products as $2N$-bit values.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{E0 (integer, sequential)}: fold with bvadd left-to-right,
      $\mathrm{acc}_i = \mathrm{bvadd}(\mathrm{acc}_{i-1}, \mathit{pp}_i)$.
      Carries propagate through the full width at each step.
\item \textbf{E1 (GF(2), sequential)}: identical topology but bvxor
      instead of bvadd: $\mathrm{acc}_i = \mathrm{bvxor}(\mathrm{acc}_{i-1}, \mathit{pp}_i)$.
      No carries.
\item \textbf{E2 (integer, parallel-tree)}: pairwise bvadd in a
      balanced tree of depth $\lceil \log_2 N \rceil$.
\item \textbf{E3 (GF(2), parallel-tree)}: same balanced tree, but
      with bvxor at each internal node.
\item \textbf{E4 (integer, ternary)}: sequential but in groups of
      three, $\mathrm{acc} = \mathrm{bvadd}(\mathrm{bvadd}(\mathrm{acc}, \mathit{pp}_i), \mathit{pp}_{i+1})$,
      making each accumulation step perform two dependent
      additions through the full carry chain.
\end{itemize}
Each variant is a macro expansion of $a \times b$ in the
commutativity assertion $a \times b = b \times a$. Because the SMT
benchmarks are identical modulo the accumulator, the SAT formulas
differ \emph{only} in the clauses encoding the accumulator gate.

Four observations:

\textbf{(1) Carry presence dominates CDCL search difficulty.} At
BW=8 integer sequential takes 6.4\,s with 184K conflicts; GF(2)
sequential solves the \emph{structurally identical} formula in
38\,ms with 2.9K conflicts---a 169$\times$ speedup and
64$\times$ fewer conflicts on formulas that differ only in
bvadd\,vs.\,bvxor. At BW$\geq$10 the integer variant times out at
120\,s while GF(2) solves in under 500\,ms (ratio $>$1000$\times$).

\textbf{(2) Topology alone does not rescue integer multiplication.}
Switching from sequential (E0) to parallel-tree (E2) accumulation
produces ${<}$10\% time difference on integer, and both time out
at the same bit-width. Parallel structure helps GF(2) by 2--5$\times$
(E1 vs E3), confirming a minor structural effect, but
structure cannot overcome carry-induced hardness.

\textbf{(3) 3-input carry has negligible effect.}
E4 (ternary integer) tracks E0 within 1\%; the solver sees a
cascade of 2-input adds regardless, so this variant does not
isolate carry depth per step from carry depth overall.

\textbf{(4) The pattern is consistent with previous encoding
data.} The ordering shift-add $<$ Dadda $<$ Comba $<$ \combacs
in Table~\ref{tab:allencodings} mirrors decreasing effective
carry propagation depth, at constant partial-product count. Combined
with the N2 experiment, this supports the claim that carry
propagation is the dominant driver of \emph{CDCL search
difficulty} for integer multiplication bit-blasting with
standard encodings.

\textbf{Scope of the claim.} The experiment establishes
that, on commutativity and on CaDiCaL, carry presence
dominates the choice of accumulation topology in determining
CDCL running time. It does \emph{not} establish a proof-theoretic
lower bound: Beame and Liew have shown that polynomial-size
regular resolution proofs of commutativity exist for array,
diagonal, and Booth multipliers~\cite{beame2019toward}, so the
hardness we observe is CDCL-search-relative, not
proof-complexity-absolute. Whether the difficulty is
intrinsic to the current generation of CDCL heuristics, or
whether better heuristics (or encoding-level guidance, as we
explore in Section~\ref{sec:combacs}) can close the gap, is
the engineering question Beame and Liew identify as open.


\section{Proof-Driven Encoding Design}
\label{sec:methodology}

\combacs was designed through a systematic methodology based on
examining solver proof traces. We describe the procedure here both
as a record of how the encoding was found and as a reproducible
technique for future encoding work.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Generate a DRAT proof} of the existing encoding on a
  representative hard benchmark (we used commutativity at BW=11
  with standard Comba).
\item \textbf{Count variable participation}: for each variable,
  count the number of proof steps in which it appears as a
  literal. Rank variables by participation count.
\item \textbf{Trace the top variables} back to the multiplier
  circuit: which partial product bits, carry bits, or intermediate
  gates do they represent?
\item \textbf{Hypothesise a structural cause} for the
  concentration: what aspect of the encoding forces the solver to
  repeatedly reason about these variables?
\item \textbf{Propose an encoding modification} that breaks the
  identified dependency without losing correctness.
\item \textbf{Validate} the modification across multiple solvers
  and benchmarks to ensure the improvement is not solver-specific.
\end{enumerate}

Applying this to standard Comba on commutativity BW=11, the top
variable by proof participation appears in 31\% of all DRAT
steps. Tracing it back to the encoding, we found it represents a
carry bit at the boundary between adjacent columns---the point
where standard Comba propagates the carry from one column's
popcount to the next during the same pass. This inter-column
dependency forces the solver to reason about carry propagation
across the entire width, creating a sequential bottleneck in an
otherwise parallel structure.

The proposed fix---\combacs---separates column reduction (first
pass) from carry propagation (second pass), eliminating the
inter-column dependency during the popcount phase. The validation
step showed: 55\% smaller proofs, 74\% more unit propagation
during search, and 2.6--71$\times$ speedup (Table~\ref{tab:proof})
across four different SAT solvers.

\paragraph{Retrospective analysis of known encodings.}
We did not design Booth radix-4, 4-bit blocks, or sorting
networks via this methodology---these are pre-existing ideas (Booth
from hardware arithmetic~\cite{booth1951signed}; 4-bit blocks
suggested by Martin Brain in private communication; sorting
networks from the synthesis literature). However, the same DRAT
analysis proves useful for \emph{understanding} why each encoding
wins or loses on specific benchmarks. Applied post-hoc to Booth on
constant multiplication, proof-variable analysis shows that Booth's
zero-valued digits for constants with few set bits eliminate entire
partial products, reducing conflicts 10$\times$
(Section~\ref{sec:alternative}). Applied to sorting networks,
analysis reveals that the 2,915 fixed variables from
sorted-property constraints are overwhelmed by the 13K extra
variables the encoding introduces.

\paragraph{Towards automation.}
The manual steps above---proof generation, variable ranking,
circuit tracing, structural hypothesis, modification---are
candidates for automation. An AI system that reads DRAT proofs,
identifies structural bottlenecks, and proposes encoding
modifications would close the loop. Existing work analyses
encoding source code~\cite{jarvisalo2012inprocessing} or modifies
solver heuristics~\cite{biere2019cadical}, but to our knowledge no
system closes the full proof-to-encoding cycle. We view our manual
use of the methodology as preliminary evidence that the cycle is
tractable; automating it is future work.

\paragraph{Development effort.}
The \combacs encoding, the six alternative encodings compared in
Section~\ref{sec:alternative}, the adaptive heuristic, and the
supporting DRAT analysis infrastructure were developed and
evaluated over ${\sim}$23 active working days with AI-assisted
pair programming (Amazon's Kiro agent;
${\sim}$200 commits, ${\sim}$8,900 lines of C++ added, across
38 source files). The agent handled routine tasks---implementing
variants of an encoding given a clear specification, writing
benchmark-runner scripts, producing tabular results, drafting
prose---quickly. Human judgment was decisive for:
(i)~formulating the structural hypothesis behind \combacs (why the
top-variable concentration in standard Comba implies a carry-save
second pass would help); (ii)~recognising that the adaptive
heuristic should disable popcount globally when multiplication
count exceeds 2, because carry-save variables block BVE cascading;
(iii)~honest scoping in the writeup (e.g., distinguishing
proof-guided \emph{design} from proof-guided \emph{analysis}, or
reporting that Booth is faster on strength-reduction but slower on
pure constant commutativity). We report these figures not as a
contribution but as a data point for future methodological studies
of AI-assisted encoding design.

\section{Carry-Save Comba Encoding}
\label{sec:combacs}

Standard Comba processes columns left-to-right, propagating carry bits
from each column's popcount to the next column during the same pass.
This creates inter-column dependencies that manifest as proof
bottlenecks: the top bottleneck variable appears in 31\% of all DRAT
proof steps.

\combacs separates the two concerns:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{First pass:} Reduce each column independently via
  popcount. Carry bits are collected but \emph{not} propagated to
  adjacent columns.
\item \textbf{Second pass:} Reduce the accumulated carry bits (which
  form much shorter columns, max height 4 even at BW=17).
\end{enumerate}

Counterintuitively, \combacs produces ${\sim}$2$\times$ more clauses
than Dadda, yet is up to 30$\times$ faster. The extra auxiliary
variables from popcount create intermediate ``summary'' variables
representing the count of 1-bits in groups of 2, 4, 8. These act as
abstractions the solver reasons about at a higher level. Formula size
is not the right optimisation target---propagation structure is.

\subsection{Adaptive Encoding Selection}

While popcount benefits commutativity checking (two identical-structure
multiplications), it hurts problems with 3+ independent multiplications
where BVE needs carry chains for cascading elimination. We measured
that shift-add achieves 106\% BVE elimination on associativity
problems---through resolution, BVE creates new variables that are
themselves eliminated in subsequent rounds, so the cumulative count
exceeds the original variable count---while carry-save encodings
achieve only 58\%.

The adaptive heuristic makes a two-way decision per multiplication.
It selects \emph{popcount} when the formula has $\leq$2 symbolic
multiplications of the same width---the commutativity pattern where
congruence closure discovers equivalent gates between identical
multiplier circuits. It selects \emph{shift-add} otherwise: when the
formula has 3+ multiplications, or when widths differ. A pre-scan
counts symbolic multiplications before encoding begins; if the count
exceeds 2, popcount is disabled globally. For constant multiplication
with few partial products, \combacs falls back to Dadda-style
carry-save reduction. The multiplication count threshold is
insensitive: changing it from 2 to 1, 4, or 99 produces identical
results because the Gr\"obner basis solver
(the algebraic solver) subsumes the threshold's purpose for
polynomial benchmarks, and non-polynomial benchmarks in our suite
have $\leq$2 multiplications.

commutativity, associativity, and distributivity benchmarks, with
the algebraic pre-solver disabled to isolate the encoding's effect:
(i) the default adaptive choice, (ii) popcount always, and
(iii) shift-add always (no carry-save at all). Table~\ref{tab:heuristic}
shows the result.

\section{Alternative Encodings}
\label{sec:alternative}

Three alternative encodings from the literature were implemented in
\cbmc and evaluated alongside \combacs: Booth radix-4 from hardware
arithmetic~\cite{booth1951signed}, 4-bit block multiplication
(suggested by Martin Brain, private communication), and sorting
networks adapted from counting-based synthesis. For each, we use
the same DRAT proof analysis that guided \combacs's design to
\emph{understand} why the encoding helps or hurts on specific
benchmark patterns. These encodings were not designed via proof
analysis; they are pre-existing ideas whose interaction with SAT
solvers we characterise here.

\subsection{Booth Radix-4}

Booth encoding~\cite{booth1951signed} examines multiplier bits in
pairs, generating partial products with values in
$\{-2, -1, 0, +1, +2\} \times$ multiplicand. This halves the number
of partial products at the cost of two's complement correction.

We initially expected Booth to excel broadly on constant
multiplication. Running a sweep over two benchmark families
(with the algebraic solver disabled to isolate the encoding
effect) gave a more nuanced picture:

Two observations. First, Booth is actually \emph{worse} on pure

(sign correction, pre-computed doublings) adds overhead that the
solver cannot simplify away when both sides are computed with
\texttt{bvmul}. Second, Booth excels on strength-reduction
benchmarks where one side uses \texttt{bvmul} and the other is a
hand-coded shift-add expression: here its compressed
representation matches the target structure more closely, and the
equivalence check becomes a few small consistency constraints
rather than a full circuit-to-circuit comparison. On the chained
benchmark strength\_chain\_16 (verifying three strength reductions
simultaneously), Booth is 17$\times$ faster than shift-add.

Proof analysis on strength\_chain\_16 supports this
interpretation: Booth's DRAT proof is 277\,KB with 4,071
conflicts, while shift-add produces ${\sim}$10\,MB of proof with
39,757 conflicts. The solver requires fundamentally less reasoning
when the encoding aligns with the target circuit.

encoding'' hierarchy.

\subsection{4-Bit Block Multiplication}

We divide both operands into 4-bit sections and compute $4 \times 4
\to 8$-bit sub-products using shift-add (small, propagation-friendly),
then accumulate block products via Dadda tree. This creates
intermediate variables at 4-bit boundaries that BVE can eliminate
efficiently: block4 achieves the highest BVE elimination rate (770
variables, 78\% of its total).

On mul\_ineq\_12 (inequality constraints), block4 is the fastest
encoding (13,617 conflicts vs.\ 15,662 for shift-add), suggesting
that the balanced tree structure from 4-bit blocks aids BVE on
problems with mixed arithmetic and comparison operations.

\subsection{Sorting Network (Negative Result)}

We compute $n^2$ partial product bits, sort each column using a
bitwise sorting network (compare-and-swap = $(x \lor y, x \land y)$),
and add auxiliary constraints enforcing the sorted property
($\mathit{out}[i] \Rightarrow \mathit{out}[i{-}1]$). The sorted
representation makes carry structure explicit.

Despite achieving the highest fixed-variable count (2,915 vs.\ 217
for shift-add)---confirming excellent propagation from the auxiliary
constraints---the encoding produces 13,316 variables and 40,462
clauses (18$\times$ and 14$\times$ more than shift-add). The 25M
propagations overwhelm the structural benefit. This is a clear
negative result: auxiliary constraints that improve propagation
locally can be net-negative when they dramatically increase formula
size.

\subsection{No Single Encoding Dominates}

The optimal encoding depends on problem structure, not just
multiplication count. This motivates encoding selection based on
deeper structural analysis of the verification problem.

\paragraph{A methodological caveat: encoding flags must reach the
solver.}
We observed an instructive phenomenon on the bfloat16 benchmark
bf16\_mul\_comm\_v2: switching among shift-add, Booth, 4-bit block,
and sorting-network encodings produces \emph{identical} CNF
formulas (1743 variables, 6697 clauses) with identical conflict
counts (417,623) and solve times (${\approx}$22\,s). Only \combacs
produces a different formula (6683 clauses, 230,430 conflicts,
11\,s). Investigating this, we found that CBMC's floating-point
path---which produces the mantissa multiplication internally---does
not propagate the alternative encoding flags (\verb|use_booth|,
\verb|use_4bit_blocks|, \verb|use_sorting_network|) to the
recursive call for mantissa multiplication. The flag
\verb|use_comba_carry_save| \emph{is} propagated because it is
also set by our adaptive heuristic at a higher level. The
consequence is that for floating-point arithmetic all encodings
except \combacs effectively fall back to the same shift-add
multiplier. This is a concrete instance of a more general
lesson: encoding experiments must verify that the flag actually
reaches every multiplier instance in the compiled formula. Fixing
the flag propagation is a small engineering change that we defer,
but it means our FP results understate the differences among
alternative encodings.

\subsection{Multi-Encoding Combinations}
\label{sec:multi-encoding}

A natural question after establishing that no single encoding
dominates is whether the solver can be given more than one
encoding at once, sharing inputs and outputs. We implemented
such a mode: \texttt{bv\_utilst::unsigned\_multiplier} accepts a
secondary encoding name; when set, the multiplier emits both
the primary encoding (selected by the existing flags) and the
secondary encoding on the same input bit-vectors, and adds the
bit-wise equality constraint between the two output bit-vectors.
The SAT formula then contains two parallel encodings with
disjoint internal variables and a shared interface. The
intuition is that each additional encoding provides another
structural handle; the risk is formula bloat and heuristic
confusion between the two alternatives.

We ran an ablation over nine benchmarks (algebraic pre-solver
disabled, \cadical, 60\,s timeout, 6\,GB memory) with every
combination of primary $\in \{$shift-add, \combacs, Booth,
block4$\}$ and secondary $\in \{$none, shift-add, \combacs,
Comba, Dadda, Booth, block4$\}$, for a total of 180 multi-encoding
runs plus 36 singleton baselines.

The outcome is \emph{portfolio-like}: multi-encoding behaves
approximately as a free portfolio of the two singletons,
paying about 30\% overhead (median) versus the best singleton
but running on median 7$\times$ faster than the worse one.
Notable rescues: on \texttt{comm\_11} shift-add alone times
out, while \texttt{shift-add+combacs} solves in 0.66\,s; on
\texttt{bf16\_mul\_comm\_v2} every combacs-containing pair
inherits the combacs speed, whereas combinations that exclude
combacs inherit the 22\,s shift-add baseline. On simple
benchmarks the overhead is visible (\texttt{comm\_8}
\texttt{block4} alone solves in 81\,ms; \texttt{block4+shift-add}
multi takes 104\,ms, a 28\% penalty), and 40\% of multi runs are
more than 1.5$\times$ slower than the better singleton, so
multi-encoding is not a universally free lunch.

The practical implication: if a user does not know which
encoding is best for a given benchmark family, a multi-encoding
configuration provides a safer default than guessing a single
encoding, at the cost of up to ${\sim}1.3\times$ overhead vs.\
the optimal choice. For the adaptive encoding-selection
heuristic in Section~\ref{sec:combacs}, multi-encoding offers
a complementary strategy when pattern recognition is not
reliable.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

We ran all experiments on an Intel Xeon Platinum 8124M (3.0\,GHz,
36 cores) with 68\,GB RAM, Ubuntu 24.04, GCC 13.3.0. Solver versions:
\cadical 3.0.0, \minisat 2.2.1, \mergesat (docker-solvers branch,
MiniSat-compatible API), \cryptominisat 5.11.21. We report median of
3 runs with a 120\,s timeout and 6\,GB memory limit. Unless otherwise
noted, we use the g-only adder encoding (the new default; see
Appendix~\ref{app:adders}).

We evaluate on 36 QF\_BV benchmarks in five categories: algebraic
identities (commutativity, overflow, strength reduction: 15
benchmarks), industrial workloads (MurmurHash3, Q15 DSP, keyed hash,
division-by-constant: 6), matrix/MAC (trace, dot product
commutativity: 4), division/FP (roundtrip, FP add/mul: 6), and
factoring (SAT instances: 6). Where explicitly noted, we extend to
44 benchmarks by adding eight DSP datapath benchmarks.

problems the algebraic layers already solve contributes nothing.

\paragraph{Context: algebraic pre-solver disabled.}
The numbers in Table~\ref{tab:allencodings} are measured with the
algebraic pre-solver (a companion paper~\cite{companion-algebraic}) disabled, which is the

pre-solver is enabled---the current \cbmc default---all polynomial

encoding, so the encoding's effect on these benchmarks is
effectively masked. Table~\ref{tab:allencodings} therefore
isolates the encoding's contribution to solver effort; in the
deployed pipeline, encoding choices matter primarily for
benchmarks the pre-solver cannot handle (bitwise operations,
inequalities, floating-point, structural circuit mismatches).

\subsection{Cross-Solver Analysis}

The four solvers differ in their response to \combacs for identifiable
reasons. \cadical benefits most (71$\times$) because it combines the
structural improvement with inprocessing BVE that exploits \combacs's
popcount intermediate variables, plus congruence closure that
identifies equivalent gates between the two multiplier circuits.
\mergesat benefits from the structural improvement alone
(2.6--$\infty\times$): as a \minisat fork with CHB activity and phase
saving but no inprocessing, the carry-save structure is sufficient to
solve comm BW=11 and BW=13 (both T/O with shift-add). \minisat's
SatELite preprocessor achieves greater simplification on the compact
shift-add formula for small problems (comm BW=9: 6.62\,s vs.\
6.67\,s), but on larger formulas the structural benefit dominates
(matrix trace: 5.39\,s where shift-add times out). Notably, \minisat
times out on comm BW=11 for \emph{all} encodings---the problem exceeds
its capacity regardless. \cryptominisat is consistently the slowest on
multiplication; its native XOR handling provides no benefit (confirmed
by running with and without XOR constraints), and it lacks both
inprocessing BVE and congruence closure.

\section{Negative Results}
\label{sec:negative}

A significant portion of this investigation produced negative results.
We report them to save future effort and because the failure modes
illuminate SAT solver behaviour. A natural first question is whether
asymptotically superior algorithms---Karatsuba~\cite{karatsuba1962multiplication}
($O(n^{1.585})$), Toom-Cook~\cite{toom1963complexity} ($O(n^{1.465})$),
or Sch\"onhage-Strassen~\cite{schonhage1971schnelle}
($O(n \log n \log \log n)$)---produce better SAT encodings.
\cbmc includes implementations of all three. They are designed for
\emph{computational} efficiency (fewer arithmetic operations), not
\emph{verification} efficiency (fewer SAT conflicts). Karatsuba's
combination step requires additions with carry chains---exactly the
structure Section~\ref{sec:hardness} identifies as the hardness source.

Karatsuba is competitive on commutativity at BW=13 but 22$\times$
slower on primality. Sch\"onhage-Strassen produces 43K variables for
BW=7 (vs.\ ${\sim}$600 for Comba)---designed for thousands of bits,
it is orders of magnitude too expensive at practical bit-widths.
Note that standard Comba loses to Dadda on aws\_mul (39.4\,s vs.\
1.29\,s), where constant multiplication dominates; \combacs's adaptive
fallback handles this.

\subsection{Approaches That Hurt Performance}

Several approaches that add structure to the encoding actually hurt
performance because they reintroduce carry chains. Radix-4/8
multipliers are 2--15$\times$ slower: pre-computing $3x$, $5x$, $7x$
adds carry chains that dominate at practical bit-widths. Full-adder
tree popcount is 3$\times$ slower than pop0 despite fewer variables,
because it creates longer carry chains ($\log_2 n$ bits vs.\ 2--4
bits). Non-propagation-complete carry merge causes a 25$\times$
regression: encoding carry with 4 clauses instead of 6 violates
propagation completeness~\cite{brain2016propagation}.

Sharing structure between multiplications also hurts: caching AND
gates between multiplications is 89\% slower because shared partial
product variables prevent independent BVE---the solver cannot
eliminate a variable that appears in clauses from both multipliers.

\subsection{Approaches With Limited or Chaotic Benefit}

XOR-based techniques consistently disappoint. We tested offline
Gaussian elimination, online incremental GE, and \cryptominisat's
built-in XOR handling. The best result was 1.4$\times$ on one
benchmark, with neutral or negative results on all others. The
problem is that \cbmc's XOR constraints are 3-variable gates---too
short for GE to combine usefully.

Solver tuning and abstraction refinement fail because the problem is
structural, not parametric. We tested 512 \cadical option
combinations (16 options $\times$ 4 encodings $\times$ 8 benchmarks);
no option changed any result by more than $\pm$3\%. \cbmc's existing
CEGAR loop for multiplication (\texttt{--refine-arithmetic}) starts
with $x \cdot 0 = 0$ and $x \cdot 1 = x$, then adds the full
multiplier on the first spurious counterexample. On UNSAT benchmarks,
the full multiplier is always needed, so the refinement loop adds 2+
solver calls of overhead (commutativity BW=11: 4.0\,s vs.\ 0.9\,s
direct \combacs). All five parallel prefix adders (BK, Kogge-Stone,
Sklansky, Han-Carlson, Ladner-Fischer) produce identical learned
clause quality (95\% glue-1~\cite{audemard2009predicting}); BK wins solely by having the fewest
variables (Appendix~\ref{app:adders}).

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Algebraic verification (brief).}
Bryant~\cite{bryant1991complexity} showed BDD-based multiplication
verification requires exponential size. Biere, Kauers, and
Ritirc~\cite{biere2017challenges}, Kaufmann~\cite{kaufmann2020thesis},
Kaufmann and Biere~\cite{kaufmann2021amulet}, and
Yu et al.~\cite{yu2016arithmetic} develop gate-level algebraic
verification of multipliers using Gr\"obner bases or function
extraction, typically over $\mathbb{Q}$ with field polynomials.
Song et al.~\cite{song2024equational} apply strong Gr\"obner bases
over $\mathbb{Z}_{2^d}$ to the equational theory of bit-vectors.
Algebraic techniques scale well with bit-width but are limited to
universal queries over polynomial fragments; they are complementary
to bit-blasting, which we treat in a companion paper~\cite{companion-algebraic}.
Clegg et al.~\cite{clegg1996groebner} proved the Gr\"obner proof
system can be exponentially more powerful than resolution,
providing theoretical justification for why algebraic approaches
can outperform SAT solvers on polynomial equation benchmarks.

\paragraph{Encoding and SAT techniques.}
Dadda~\cite{dadda1965some} and Wallace~\cite{wallace1964suggestion}
proposed tree-based partial product reduction.
Comba~\cite{comba1990exponentiation} proposed column-wise reduction.
\combacs extends Comba with carry-save separation, optimised for SAT
solving rather than hardware implementation.
Howe et al.~\cite{howe2025subpolynomial} decompose univariate binary
polynomials into subpolynomials by fixing input bits one at a time,
giving compact bit-blasting encodings for polynomial evaluation.
Their technique is developed for univariate polynomials; extending
it to multivariate polynomials (such as $a \times b$) yields
structure close to the standard partial-product grid.
E\'en and Biere~\cite{een2005effective} introduced SatELite
preprocessing with BVE. Biere et al.~\cite{biere2019cadical} extended
this to inprocessing in \cadical. Our analysis
(Appendix~\ref{sec:bve}) reveals that inprocessing BVE can be
counterproductive for specific encoding structures.
Brain et al.~\cite{brain2016propagation} showed that PC encodings
compose for adders but not multipliers.
Brain~\cite{brain2021wrong} conjectured that a PC multiplier
is exponential, and explored Toom-Cook polynomial interpolation as
an alternative approximation strategy. Martin Brain suggested the
4-bit block encoding idea explored in Section~\ref{sec:alternative}
in private communication; we implemented and evaluated it.
Jia et al.~\cite{jia2023improving} improve bit-blasting for
nonlinear integer arithmetic (QF\_NIA) with Greedy Addition (a
Huffman-like ordering that minimises auxiliary variables) and
Multiplication Adaptation (starting with narrow bit-widths). We
tested Greedy Addition across four solvers and found mixed results:
\cadical benefits (1.4--2.6$\times$ faster) because its inprocessing
BVE works better with balanced trees, but \minisat and \mergesat
regress (up to 1.8$\times$ slower) because their preprocessing BVE
prefers long sequential carry chains. This solver-dependent effect
reinforces our finding that encoding structure interacts deeply with
solver-specific techniques.
Fazekas et al.~\cite{fazekas2025incremental} extend incremental
inprocessing to support Bounded Variable Addition (BVA), the dual of
BVE. We found BVA catastrophic for multiplication (all benchmarks T/O
when enabled in \cadical). Our g-only technique
(Appendix~\ref{app:adders}) is superficially similar but fundamentally
different: g-only adds AND gates that \emph{enable} BVE, while BVA
adds variables that \emph{prevent} it.
Encoding techniques for SAT have been studied
extensively~\cite{tseitin1983complexity,plaisted1986structure}.

\paragraph{Resolution upper bounds for ring identities.}
Beame and Liew~\cite{beame2017towards,beame2019toward} refute
an earlier conjecture that multiplier commutativity requires
exponential-size resolution proofs: they give polynomial-size
regular resolution proofs for any degree-2 ring identity
(commutativity, distributivity) on array, diagonal, and Booth
multipliers, and quasi-polynomial-size ($n^{O(\log n)}$) proofs
for Wallace tree multipliers. Their construction splits the
multiplier formula into narrow ``critical strips'' and uses
a branching program (read-once BDD) that scans each strip
in parallel; they note explicitly that current CDCL solvers
do not find these short proofs and call it an important open
problem to add the right guiding information to help them.

\paragraph{Escaping resolution hardness in CDCL.}
Three complementary lines of work address the gap between
existence of short proofs and CDCL's ability to find them.
(i)~Encoding-level guidance---our approach---reshapes the
initial formula so CDCL's standard heuristics reach short
proofs. (ii)~Solver-level generalisation to stronger proof
systems: Danner's dissertation~\cite{danner2025sat} develops
CDXCL, a CDCL generalisation to XOR-OR-AND Normal Form
(XNF) clauses that operates in the Res($\oplus$) proof
system---equivalent to Itsykson and Sokolov's SRES---and
that provably escapes Resolution lower bounds on Tseitin
formulas and related XOR-rich instances.
(iii)~Inprocessing-level reshaping: Pollitt et
al.~\cite{pollitt2026factoring} revive Extended Resolution
inside modern CDCL through CaDiCaL-ER (definition introduction
during conflict analysis) and CaDiCaL-FX (bounded variable
addition extended to XOR and ITE gate detection on learned
clauses). They further give polynomial-size ER proofs for
Tseitin formulas via XOR gate factorisation followed by
bounded variable elimination. Our encoding-selection work is
orthogonal to both: we shape the CNF that is fed to CDCL,
whereas CDXCL changes the proof system and CaDiCaL-FX
reshapes clauses during search.

\paragraph{Word-level solvers.}
Niemetz et al.~\cite{niemetz2023bitwuzla} implement word-level
normalisation in Bitwuzla that avoids bit-blasting for algebraic
identities; cvc5 uses a similar rewriting infrastructure. These
approaches are complementary to our encoding improvements: when
word-level rewriting succeeds, bit-blasting is not needed; when it
does not (bitwise operations, non-polynomial constraints), the
encoding matters. Chukharev et al.~\cite{chukharev2025partitioning}
show that circuit-structure-aware SAT partitioning can solve
multiplier equivalence checking instances intractable for both
standard SAT and algebraic methods, complementary to our approach.


\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

We presented a proof-guided methodology for SAT encoding design:
analyse DRAT proofs to identify structural bottlenecks, then redesign
the encoding to eliminate them. This methodology produced \combacs
(2.6--71$\times$ speedup on commutativity) and guided our evaluation
of Booth radix-4 (up to 17$\times$ on strength-reduction benchmarks),
4-bit
blocks, and sorting networks.

Our analysis establishes that carry propagation is the dominant
driver of \emph{CDCL search difficulty} for integer multiplication
commutativity on standard encodings, via a controlled experiment
varying only the accumulator operator on identical partial-product
layouts; structural variations change running time by less than
$5\times$ while carry presence changes it by orders of magnitude.
Beame and Liew~\cite{beame2019toward} have shown that
polynomial-size regular resolution proofs of commutativity on
array, diagonal, and Booth multipliers exist; our empirical
result therefore establishes that the gap between \emph{provable
existence} of short proofs and \emph{CDCL's ability to find them}
is where the practical difficulty lies. The encoding choices we
investigate can be understood as adding problem-specific
guidance that helps CDCL find the short proofs that are known to
exist. No single encoding dominates all problem classes. The
optimal encoding depends on problem structure: commutativity
benefits from congruence closure (\combacs), constant
multiplication from fewer partial products (Booth), and
circuit equivalence from structural matching (shift-add).

Several directions remain open. First, the proof-guided methodology
could be automated: an AI system that reads DRAT proofs, identifies
bottlenecks, and proposes encoding modifications would close the loop
we perform manually. Second, the encoding selection heuristic could
be extended beyond multiplication count to consider deeper structural
properties (symmetry, constant operands, comparison targets). Third,
propagating encoding flags through the floating-point path would
enable Booth and block4 to benefit FP verification. Fourth,
whether CDCL heuristics can be steered to find the polynomial-size
proofs guaranteed by~\cite{beame2019toward} without the encoding
guidance we provide is an important open problem: a positive
answer would make our encoding-selection layer unnecessary, a
negative answer would strengthen the case for encoding-aware
SAT solving. Recent work in two complementary directions---
solver-level generalisation to stronger proof systems
(Danner~\cite{danner2025sat}, and work on CDCL with XOR reasoning
that bidirectionally simulates Res($\oplus$)) and
inprocessing-level definition introduction
(Pollitt et al.~\cite{pollitt2026factoring})---suggests that the
broader research programme is active and that encoding-level,
solver-level, and inprocessing-level guidance can be combined.

Adding redundant AND gates ($g[i] = a[i] \wedge b[i]$) to the
ripple-carry adder triggers BVE polarity alignment cascades: the AND
gate's clauses structurally match carry generation clauses, enabling
BVE to eliminate them trivially, which reduces occurrence counts of
input variables, enabling further cascading elimination.

g-only is applied only to \emph{top-level} additions (equality checks,
explicit \texttt{bvadd}). Multiplier-internal additions are isolated
by the adder encoding swap in \texttt{unsigned\_multiplier} and always
use ripple-carry.

\subsection{Division and Floating-Point}

through to shift-add via \combacs's sparse-constant fallback
($>$32 bits with few partial products); (3)~the FP barrel shifter and rounding logic dominate
solving time.

safety margin for FP mantissa multiplication (48 bits).


\section{BVE Interaction}
\label{sec:bve}

\cadical's inprocessing BVE is \emph{counterproductive} for \combacs
at BW$\geq$11, adding 4--140\% overhead. Disabling BVE
(\texttt{elim=0}) gives 1.1--2.3$\times$ additional speedup. The
carry-save structure creates popcount intermediate variables that BVE
attempts to eliminate, but the resulting resolvents are harder than the
original clauses. We do not disable BVE by default because the
adaptive heuristic routes many multiplications to shift-add where
BVE is beneficial.

Tracing clause creation and deletion in \cadical reveals that the
latest-learned clauses encode carry propagation relationships between
adjacent equality check bits. For commutativity ($c = a \times b$,
$d = b \times a$, assert $c = d$), the equality check creates XNOR
gates $\mathit{eq}[i] = c[i] \Leftrightarrow d[i]$. The late-learned
clauses are of the form $(\mathit{eq}[i] \vee \mathit{eq}[i{+}1])$:
``if bit $i$ differs, the adjacent bit must agree.''

Pre-providing these as encoding clauses achieves 18--52\% speedup at
BW=10--13. However, the effect is non-monotonic: +59\% regression at
BW=14 and +94\% at BW=16. This is an instance of CDCL sensitivity to
clause perturbation~\cite{elffers2018trade}: the hints change BVE's
elimination order, and the residual problem's difficulty is a
non-monotonic function of that order. The hints are enabled in \cbmc
for 10--13 bit equality checks only (below 10, the effect is
negligible; above 13, regressions dominate).
